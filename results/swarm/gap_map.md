  - latest new record seen: `8867 -> 8884`
- That already argues against the lazy story that the sequence "looks bounded by a small constant" from the opening terms alone.

## Prioritized negative space

### 1. Frontier coverage certificates for long skipped intervals

**Priority:** highest

**Gap.**
There is still no useful criterion for deciding, near the moving frontier, whether an integer is already in the product set generated by the current row/column prefixes or must become the next border term.

**Why this is under-served.**
The surfaced sources document the construction and examples, but not a proof framework for the only local event that matters: a whole interval being covered immediately before the next uncovered row-1 term.

**Concrete research target.**
For the border sets
- `A_n = {T(1,1), ..., T(1,n)}`
- `B_n = {T(1,1), ..., T(n,1)}`

build a usable certificate for when every integer in `[m+1, m+L]` already lies in `A_n B_n`, and identify what extra condition forces `m+L+1` onto row 1 rather than column 1.

**Why it matters.**
Boundedness of the row-1 difference sequence is almost the same as saying these fully covered frontier intervals cannot grow arbitrarily long. Right now there is no language for proving that.

**Concrete evidence from the local run.**
At the record gap `8867 -> 8884`, every skipped value `8868..8883` already had a witness in the current product set.

### 2. Prime-side assignment dynamics are not understood

**Priority:** high

**Gap.**
It is known empirically and in OEIS notes that every prime lands on exactly one axis, but there is no clean rule for which prime goes to row 1 versus column 1, nor for how those assignments cluster locally.

**Why this is under-served.**
The problem is often narrated in prime language, but large row-1 gaps are not controlled by prime gaps alone. They are controlled by whether nearby primes have already been claimed by the opposite axis and whether the surrounding composites are already representable.

**Concrete research target.**
Study the deterministic rule that assigns a prime `p` to row 1 or column 1, then ask:
- can one predict the side from earlier factorization data?
- do same-axis prime clusters create unusually long future row gaps?
- is there any residue-class or multiplicative-shadow heuristic that survives computation?

**Why it matters.**
A proof strategy centered only on prime density or ordinary prime-gap heuristics is likely mis-aimed.

**Concrete evidence from the local run.**
Several record gaps each contained exactly one prime in the skipped interval, and that prime had already been assigned to column 1:
- `143..149` contains skipped prime `149`
- `333..342` contains skipped prime `337`
- `2924..2934` contains skipped prime `2927`
- `3569..3580` contains skipped prime `3571`

### 3. Local overlap, not global density, is the missing statistic

**Priority:** high

**Gap.**
Almost any global counting argument is too coarse. The product set `A_n B_n` has heavy multiplicative collisions, so `|A_n||B_n|` says very little about whether the frontier is locally saturated.

**Why this is under-served.**
The tempting move is to argue from density, average coverage, or an "about `n^2` products" heuristic. That loses the one feature that decides the conjecture: how many consecutive integers right below the mex are covered, and how fragile those coverings are.

**Concrete research target.**
Measure frontier-local multiplicative energy:
- how many witness factorizations does a typical skipped value near a record gap have?
- do long gaps require many redundant witnesses, or only one witness per integer?
- can one separate stable coverage from brittle `1 * p` or `2 * q` coverage?

**Why it matters.**
If long gaps are sustained by a few brittle witnesses, a proof or counterexample will look very different than if they require robust overlap.

**Concrete evidence from the local run.**
Both behaviors appear:
- redundant coverage: `333 = 9 * 37 = 111 * 3`, `8880 = 15 * 592 = 296 * 30 = 370 * 24`
- brittle coverage: `337 = 1 * 337`, `334 = 2 * 167`

### 4. There is no extremal growth law for record gaps

**Priority:** medium-high

**Gap.**
No serious public extremal map surfaced for
- `M(N) = max_{k <= N} (T(1,k+1) - T(1,k))`

so even the empirical regime is poorly described.

**Why this is under-served.**
OEIS gives terms, not record-gap statistics. The repo's literature artifacts do not recover relevant papers, and the low-budget external pass did not surface a proof, a disproof, or even a standard heuristic growth model.

**Concrete research target.**
Push computation far past the current small examples and log, for each new record:
- location
- gap size
- witness profile of each skipped integer
- prime/composite mix in the skipped interval

Then test bounded, slowly divergent, and rare-jump hypotheses separately rather than collapsing them into one guess.

**Why it matters.**
The local run already shows new records arriving late:
- gap `12` at step `720`
- gap `13` at step `866`
- gap `17` at step `2063`

That is enough to kill small-data complacency, but not enough to distinguish boundedness from very slow divergence.

### 5. Robustness under nearby greedy rules is almost unmapped

**Priority:** medium

**Gap.**
It is unclear whether boundedness, if true, is a rigid property of this exact mex-product update rule or an artifact of a narrow choice of order and exclusions.

**Why this is under-served.**
This problem has an unusually specific co-evolving frontier. Nearby variants are not a distraction here; they are one of the few ways to discover which invariants are real and which are accidental.

**Concrete under-explored settings.**
- swap the order of choosing the new row and column terms
- choose both new axis terms from the same snapshot with a symmetric tie rule
- forbid trivial `1 * p` witness usage when diagnosing prime placement
- replace multiplication with a nearby multiplicative operation such as `lcm`

**Concrete research target.**
Map which variants preserve:
- prime separation across the two axes
- outer-product structure
- apparently small early differences
- late record-gap behavior

**Why it matters.**
If the bounded-gap behavior disappears under tiny perturbations, then a successful proof of boundedness for the original rule will probably need a very specific invariant, not a broad density heuristic.

## What To De-prioritize

- Plain prime-gap analogies: they miss skipped primes already assigned to the opposite axis.
- Naive `n^2` coverage counting: multiplicative collisions are too heavy.
- Asymptotic balance arguments by themselves: even a `50/50` split of numbers or primes does not control the worst local frontier interval.

## Recommended next moves

1. Build a reusable computation notebook or script that records full witness data for every new record gap.
2. Treat prime-side assignment as a first-class object, not a side observation.
3. Search for a frontier certificate or obstruction language before attempting another density-style proof.
4. Run one or two nearby variants to see whether late record gaps are robust or fragile.
