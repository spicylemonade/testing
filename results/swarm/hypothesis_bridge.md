# Cross-Domain Bridge Hypotheses for Arithmetic Kakeya via Cellular Automata

## 1. Critical-Droplet Anisotropic Forcing

- **Closest prior art:** Cosmin Pohoata and Dmitrii Zakharov, *Generalized Arithmetic Kakeya* (2024); Bela Bollobas, Hugo Duminil-Copin, Robert Morris, and Paul Smith, *Universality for two-dimensional critical cellular automata* (2014); Ivailo Hartarsky and Tamas Robert Mezei, *Complexity of 2D bootstrap percolation difficulty* (2018).
- **Why it is different:** This does not just rename the forcing process as bootstrap percolation. The bridge claim is that legal `X`-constructible graphs can be compiled from anisotropic monotone CA rules, and that the imported CA notion of critical droplet difficulty is the right search objective for choosing sparse `f_i`, `R`, and `T` that still force every vertex.
- **Falsifiable prediction:** If this bridge is real, then exhaustive search over radius-2 anisotropic rules translated into legal constructible gadgets will show a clear advantage over isotropic and random controls, and at least one Toom-like one-sided family will produce verified scores trending below `1.675`. If no anisotropic family beats `1.70` by `n <= 10^5`, this bridge should be abandoned.
- **Required experiments:** Build a compiler from a monotone CA rule to legal `X,G,R,T`; enumerate radius-1 and radius-2 anisotropic rules and rank them by difficulty or a computable proxy; compare verified scores against isotropic and random label controls at matched `|X|`, degree, and `n`.

## 2. Multispecies Abelian-Network Forcing

- **Closest prior art:** Ben Green and Imre Ruzsa, *On the arithmetic Kakeya conjecture of Katz and Tao* (2017); Benjamin Bond and Lionel Levine, *Abelian networks I. Foundations and examples* (2013), *II. Halting on all inputs* (2014), and *III. The critical group* (2014).
- **Why it is different:** Existing abelian-network work studies local commutativity, halting, and critical groups for distributed automata, but not the specific `\mathbb{Z}^2`-valued forcing relation used here. The bridge is to reinterpret each nonzero element of `X` as a chip species and use least-action and Smith-normal-form invariants to preselect constructible families that can isolate `(1,-1)` at single vertices unusually cheaply.
- **Falsifiable prediction:** For sampled hierarchical families, full forcing will occur only when the weighted critical group contains a class aligned with the target direction `(1,-1)` and the associated production matrix is halting; this filter should retain nearly all sub-`1.70` candidates while discarding most random families. If target-direction torsion has no predictive value for success or score up to `n ≈ 5000`, reject this bridge.
- **Required experiments:** Define the exact abelian-network encoding of labels, edge moves, and the reveal rule; compute production matrices, Smith normal forms, and target-direction invariant factors for sampled constructible families; test whether these invariants predict verified forcing completion and score better than naive edge-density or degree heuristics.

## 3. Integer-Lifted Tanner Gadgets with Local CA Decoders

- **Closest prior art:** Michael Sipser and Daniel Spielman, *Expander Codes* (1996); Brett Hemenway, Rafail Ostrovsky, and Mary Wootters, *Local Correctability of Expander Codes* (2013); Aleksander Kubica and John Preskill, *Cellular-automaton decoders with provable thresholds for topological codes* (2018).
- **Why it is different:** Coding-theory decoders typically work over finite-field syndromes on Tanner graphs, while arithmetic Kakeya forcing works over integer vector labels on hierarchical constructible graphs. The bridge is to compile sparse expander or Tanner checks into small legal constructible blocks and decode them with a purely local peeling or sweep cellular automaton, so that decoder success is literally equivalent to deriving `(1,-1)` everywhere.
- **Falsifiable prediction:** Irregular expander-based gadget families will exhibit a sharp local-decoder threshold in seed density and outperform degree-matched grid and random controls by at least `0.05` in verified score, with the best family crossing `1.675`. If performance matches matched random controls or if local-tree threshold predictions do not correlate with observed forcing, reject this bridge.
- **Required experiments:** Build a gadget compiler from sparse Tanner checks to legal constructible graphs with a small integer label set `X`; implement a purely local asynchronous peeling or sweep decoder and verify that successful decoding is equivalent to full forcing; sample expander ensembles and matched controls, then compare threshold location, stopping-set size, and final verified score.
