% Abstract
% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
We present the design, implementation, and evaluation of a high-performance
DEFLATE decompressor written in portable C targeting x86-64 hardware. Through
a sequence of six cumulative optimizations---an 11-bit primary Huffman lookup
table, a 64-bit branchless bit reader, multi-literal decoding, and a packed
table-entry format with saved-bitbuffer extraction---our decoder achieves a
geometric mean throughput of 2{,}307~MB/s across 60 benchmark configurations
(20~files $\times$ 3~compression levels), representing a 1.63$\times$ speedup
over system zlib~1.2.x. With profile-guided optimization (PGO), throughput
rises to 2{,}610~MB/s (1.89$\times$ zlib). At compression level~6 alone, the
decoder reaches 2{,}414~MB/s (1.75$\times$ zlib). The decoder is fully RFC~1951
compliant, passes 945~combined correctness and adversarial tests, and is clean
under AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer. A detailed ablation study
reveals that two optimizations account for over 95\% of the total 6.9$\times$
speedup over the naive baseline: the 11-bit table with 64-bit branchless
bitreader (+391\%) and packed entries with saved-bitbuffer extraction (+31.6\%).
We further analyze the fundamental serial dependency chain in Huffman decoding
that limits all single-threaded, standard-DEFLATE decoders to a theoretical
ceiling of approximately 3--4~GB/s at current clock frequencies, and document
five attempted optimizations that produced zero or negative throughput gains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

DEFLATE~\citep{rfc1951} is the most widely deployed lossless compression format
in computing. It underlies gzip, zlib, PNG, ZIP, HTTP content encoding, and
dozens of archival and transfer protocols. Despite its age---the specification
dates to 1996---DEFLATE decompression remains a performance-critical operation in
web servers, databases, file systems, and data pipelines. A single percentage
point of throughput improvement translates to measurable gains at the scale of
modern infrastructure.

The standard library implementation, zlib, prioritizes correctness and
portability over raw speed. Modern alternatives---zlib-ng~\citep{zlibng2013},
libdeflate~\citep{biggers2017libdeflate}, and Intel ISA-L~\citep{intel2014isal}%
---have demonstrated that 2--3$\times$ speedups over zlib are achievable through
wider Huffman tables, packed decode entries, SIMD-accelerated memory copies, and
architecture-specific instruction selection. However, the techniques underlying
these gains are not well documented in the academic literature. Implementation
details are scattered across source code comments, blog
posts~\citep{giesen2018readingbits, johnson2022fasterzlib}, and optimization
guides~\citep{fog2024microarch}.

The core bottleneck in DEFLATE decoding is the \emph{serial Huffman table
lookup}: each symbol depends on the previous symbol's bit length to determine
where the next symbol begins in the bitstream. This loop-carried dependency
chain of 6--7 cycles per symbol (on modern x86-64 hardware) sets a fundamental
throughput ceiling that cannot be broken without modifying the bitstream format.
Our goal was to design a decoder that approaches this ceiling using a
combination of techniques: speculative multi-symbol decoding, SIMD-accelerated
literal runs, precomputed decode tables, and careful microarchitectural
optimization.

This paper describes a from-scratch DEFLATE decompressor designed to explore the
performance envelope of single-threaded, software-only decompression on commodity
x86-64 hardware. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A systematic ablation study isolating the throughput impact of each major
    optimization in a DEFLATE decoder (Section~\ref{sec:results:ablation}).
  \item A detailed description of the packed table-entry format with
    saved-bitbuffer extraction that yields a 31.6\% throughput gain
    (Section~\ref{sec:method:packed}).
  \item An empirical catalog of optimizations that \emph{do not work},
    including 12-bit tables, 4-literal cascades, and parallel block decoding
    (Section~\ref{sec:discussion:negative}).
  \item An analysis of the serial dependency chain and the feasibility of
    breaking it within the standard DEFLATE format
    (Section~\ref{sec:discussion:serial}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{High-performance DEFLATE libraries.}
libdeflate~\citep{biggers2017libdeflate} is the fastest known single-threaded,
standard-DEFLATE decompressor. It employs 11-bit primary tables, packed 32-bit
table entries, a 64-bit OR-shift bit reader, multi-literal decoding (up to~3
per refill), and BMI2 instructions (\texttt{BZHI}, \texttt{SHRX}) for
branchless bit extraction. zlib-ng~\citep{zlibng2013} modernizes zlib with
SIMD-accelerated Adler32/CRC32 checksums (SSE4.2, AVX2), an improved inflate
loop, and a 9-bit primary literal/length table. Intel
ISA-L~\citep{intel2014isal} provides hand-tuned NASM assembly with 12-bit
primary tables, 3-symbol packed entries, and speculative literal writes.

\paragraph{Huffman decoding theory.}
Huffman's original construction~\citep{huffman1952method} and canonical code
variants form the basis of all DEFLATE decoders. Hirschberg and
Lelewer~\citep{hirschberg1990efficient} established the table-lookup approach to
prefix-code decoding, analyzing the tradeoff between table width and subtable
lookup frequency. Belu and Coltuc~\citep{belu2022fast} proposed a fast canonical
Huffman decoder optimized for hardware implementation.

\paragraph{Bit-reader design.}
Giesen~\citep{giesen2018readingbits} provides a comprehensive survey of
bit-reader implementations, analyzing tradeoffs between refill strategies
(byte-at-a-time, word-at-a-time, branchless OR-shift). Johnson's work on
zero-refill-latency bit reading~\citep{johnson2022zerorefill} demonstrates
techniques for overlapping refill latency with table lookup on Apple~M1 and
x86 architectures. Collet's analysis of Huff0
decoding~\citep{collet2015huff0} describes multi-symbol decode and interleaved
entropy streams in the context of finite-state entropy codecs.

\paragraph{Parallel and GPU-based approaches.}
GPU-based parallel decompression has been explored by Weissenberger and
Schmidt~\citep{weissenberger2018massively} for Huffman codes,
Takafuji et~al.~\citep{takafuji2022gpu} for DEFLATE on GPUs, and
Rivera et~al.~\citep{rivera2022optimizing} for lossy compression pipelines.
NVIDIA's GDeflate~\citep{uralsky2022gdeflate} achieves 10--40$\times$ CPU
throughput by partitioning the stream into independently decodable 64\,KB tiles,
but this requires a non-standard format. CPU-based parallel approaches include
pugz~\citep{pugz2019} and rapidgzip~\citep{knespel2023rapidgzip}, which exploit
multi-block gzip streams for thread-level parallelism. Johnson's work on
synchronizing non-self-synchronizing prefix
codes~\citep{johnson2022parallelising} proposes convergence-based parallel
decoding but requires format-specific heuristics.

\paragraph{Hardware accelerators.}
Satpathy et~al.~\citep{satpathy2018deflate} describe a 14\,nm CMOS DEFLATE
decompression accelerator achieving 34.4\,Mbps at 1.56\,Tbps/W.
Zhang et~al.~\citep{zhang2024inflate} present a 43.3\,bit/cycle accelerator
with multi-checkpoint Huffman decoding for hyperscale data centers.

\paragraph{Microarchitectural references.}
Fog's instruction tables~\citep{fog2024instrtables} and microarchitecture
guide~\citep{fog2024microarch} provide the instruction latencies and throughput
numbers used in our critical-path analysis.

\paragraph{Compression fundamentals.}
DEFLATE combines LZ77~\citep{ziv1977universal} back-references with Huffman
coding. The LZ77 component introduces a 32\,KB sliding window and
variable-length match copies that constitute 30--60\% of symbols in typical
compressed streams.

\section{Background and Preliminaries}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The DEFLATE Format}
\label{sec:background:deflate}

DEFLATE~\citep{rfc1951} compresses data using a combination of
LZ77~\citep{ziv1977universal} back-references and Huffman
coding~\citep{huffman1952method}. A compressed stream consists of one or more
\emph{blocks}, each of which is one of three types: \emph{stored}
(uncompressed), \emph{fixed-Huffman} (using a predefined code), or
\emph{dynamic-Huffman} (with a per-block Huffman tree serialized in the block
header).

Dynamic blocks begin with a serialized code-length table from which the decoder
reconstructs two Huffman trees: a \emph{literal/length tree} (up to 286 symbols)
and a \emph{distance tree} (up to 32 symbols). Symbols~0--255 represent literal
bytes; symbol~256 signals end-of-block; symbols~257--285 encode match lengths
(3--258 bytes) with 0--5 extra bits. Distance codes encode offsets
(1--32{,}768 bytes) with 0--13 extra bits.

\subsection{Table-Based Huffman Decoding}
\label{sec:background:table}

Direct tree traversal decodes one bit per cycle and is prohibitively slow.
Modern decoders use \emph{table-based lookup}: the next $k$ bits of the input
are used as an index into a precomputed table that returns the decoded symbol
and its code length in a single memory
access~\citep{hirschberg1990efficient}. If the actual code length exceeds $k$
bits, a secondary subtable is consulted. The choice of $k$ involves a tradeoff
between table size (which must fit in L1~cache) and subtable lookup frequency.

The DEFLATE literal/length alphabet contains codes up to 15 bits. With an
11-bit primary table ($2^{11} = 2{,}048$ entries $\times$ 4 bytes = 8\,KB),
codes of length $\leq 11$ are resolved in a single lookup. Remaining codes
(12--15 bits) require a secondary subtable of at most $2^4 = 16$ entries per
primary slot. An 11-bit table fits comfortably in the 32--48\,KB L1 data cache
of all modern x86-64 processors~\citep{fog2024microarch}.

\subsection{The Serial Dependency Problem}
\label{sec:background:serial}

The decode loop for a DEFLATE block has the following critical path per symbol:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Refill bit buffer from input stream (1--2 cycles).
  \item Mask and index into Huffman table (1 cycle).
  \item L1 data cache load (4--5 cycles on x86-64)~\citep{fog2024microarch}.
  \item Extract symbol value and code length (1 cycle).
  \item Shift bit buffer by code length (1 cycle).
\end{enumerate}

Steps 3 through~5 form a \emph{loop-carried dependency} of 6--7 cycles: the
shift amount in step~5 determines the index bits for the next iteration's
step~2. Out-of-order execution can overlap refill and output operations with
this chain, but it cannot break it. At 6--7 cycles per symbol and an average of
$\sim$1.5 bytes per symbol (for typical text compressed at level~6), the
theoretical ceiling is roughly 3--4\,GB/s at 4\,GHz. Observed throughput is
lower due to branch mispredictions, subtable lookups, and back-reference copy
overhead.

This serial dependency is inherent to the DEFLATE format and cannot be
eliminated without modifying the bitstream. GPU-parallel approaches like
GDeflate~\citep{uralsky2022gdeflate} circumvent it by partitioning the stream
into independently decodable tiles, but this requires a non-standard format.
Parallel CPU approaches~\citep{johnson2022parallelising,
knespel2023rapidgzip, pugz2019} attempt to find synchronization points within
standard streams but face the constraint that approximately 50\% of real-world
DEFLATE streams consist of a single block (see Section~\ref{sec:discussion:serial}).

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Our decoder implements the complete RFC~1951 specification, including stored
blocks, fixed Huffman blocks, dynamic Huffman blocks with code-length decoding,
and all edge cases (zero-length codes, single-symbol alphabets,
maximum-distance back-references). The following subsections describe each
optimization in the order it was applied.

\subsection{11-Bit Primary Table with Secondary Subtables}
\label{sec:method:table}

We use a 2{,}048-entry primary lookup table indexed by the low 11~bits of the
bit buffer. Each entry is a 32-bit packed value encoding either a decoded result
(for codes $\leq 11$ bits) or a pointer to a secondary subtable (for codes of
12--15 bits). Subtables are allocated contiguously after the primary table.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Primary table lookup with subtable fallback.},label=lst:tablelookup]
index = bitbuf & 0x7FF;          /* 11 low bits */
entry = table[index];
if (entry & EXCEPTIONAL_FLAG) {  /* subtable pointer */
    sub_idx  = entry >> 16;
    sub_bits = (entry >> 8) & 0x3F;
    bitbuf >>= 11;
    entry = table[sub_idx + (bitbuf & ((1 << sub_bits) - 1))];
}
\end{lstlisting}

The primary table (8\,KB) and typical subtable allocations ($< 2$\,KB) total
under 10\,KB, fitting in L1 data cache on all modern x86-64
microarchitectures~\citep{fog2024microarch}.

\subsection{64-Bit Branchless Bit Reader}
\label{sec:method:bitreader}

Our bit reader maintains a 64-bit accumulator (\texttt{uint64\_t bits}) and a
count of valid bits (\texttt{int nbits}). Refill is performed by a single
unaligned 64-bit load, OR-shifted into position:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Branchless 64-bit bit-buffer refill.},label=lst:bitreader]
uint64_t word;
memcpy(&word, ptr, 8);  /* compiles to movq on x86-64 */
bits  |= word << nbits;
unsigned bytes = (63 - nbits) >> 3;
ptr   += bytes;
nbits += bytes << 3;
\end{lstlisting}

This refill is branchless on the fast path and guarantees $\geq 56$ valid bits
after execution. The technique is described by
Giesen~\citep{giesen2018readingbits} and is used in both libdeflate and
zlib-ng.

\subsection{Multi-Literal Decode (3-Literal Cascade)}
\label{sec:method:multiliteral}

After decoding one literal, if sufficient bits remain in the accumulator ($\geq
11$ bits), we immediately attempt a second table lookup without refilling. If
the second result is also a literal, we attempt a third. Only after the third
literal (or upon encountering a non-literal) do we refill.

This reduces refill frequency by up to 3$\times$ on literal-heavy data. The
cascade is bounded at 3 because a 56-bit buffer can hold at most $\lfloor
56/7 \rfloor = 8$ minimum-length codes, but the branch prediction cost of
deeper cascades outweighs the refill savings (Section~\ref{sec:discussion:negative}).

\subsection{Packed Entry Format with Saved-Bitbuffer Extraction}
\label{sec:method:packed}

This is the single most impactful optimization beyond the initial table and
bitreader design, contributing a 31.6\% marginal throughput gain.

\paragraph{Problem.}
A naive decoder, upon encountering a length code, must: (1)~extract the base
length from a lookup table, (2)~read the extra-bit count from another table,
(3)~extract extra bits from the bitstream, (4)~compute the final length, then
repeat for the distance code. This involves four dependent array accesses on the
hot path.

\paragraph{Solution.}
We pack all decode information into a single 32-bit table entry:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Literal entry:} Sign bit set (bit~31 = 1, testable via
    \texttt{(int32\_t)entry < 0}), literal byte in bits~23:16, code length in
    bits~3:0.
  \item \textbf{Length/distance entry:} Sign bit clear, base value in
    bits~24:16, Huffman code length only in bits~11:8, total bits to consume
    (code length + extra bits) in bits~4:0.
  \item \textbf{End-of-block entry:} Exceptional flag in bit~15, end-of-block
    marker in bit~13.
\end{itemize}

The key insight is the \emph{saved-bitbuffer technique}: before consuming bits
for a symbol, we snapshot the current bit buffer. The extra bits for
length/distance codes are located immediately above the Huffman code bits in
this snapshot. We extract them with a single shift-and-mask:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Saved-bitbuffer extraction for length/distance codes.},label=lst:savedbitbuf]
saved_bitbuf = bits;             /* snapshot before consume */
bits >>= TOTAL_BITS(entry);     /* consume code + extra */
nbits -= TOTAL_BITS(entry);
/* For length/distance entries: */
value = BASE_VALUE(entry)
      + ((saved_bitbuf & BITMASK(TOTAL_BITS(entry)))
         >> CODE_LEN_ONLY(entry));
\end{lstlisting}

This replaces four dependent array accesses with a single table lookup and three
ALU operations (shift, mask, add). The technique was pioneered by
libdeflate~\citep{biggers2017libdeflate} and described in general terms by
Giesen~\citep{giesen2022oodlehuffman}.

\subsection{SSE2/AVX2 SIMD Match Copy}
\label{sec:method:simd}

Back-reference copies are dispatched by distance to minimize overhead using a
tiered strategy:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Distance $\geq$ 32, length $\geq$ 32:} AVX2
    \texttt{\_mm256\_storeu\_si256} loop (32 bytes/iteration).
  \item \textbf{Distance $\geq$ 16, length $\geq$ 16:} SSE2
    \texttt{\_mm\_storeu\_si128} loop (16 bytes/iteration).
  \item \textbf{Distance $\geq$ 8:} 8-byte \texttt{memcpy} loop.
  \item \textbf{Distance = 1:} \texttt{memset} (RLE special case).
  \item \textbf{Distance 2--7:} Word-size copy loops with overlap handling.
\end{itemize}

During the match copy, we preload the next literal/length table entry to overlap
the copy latency with the subsequent decode, exploiting instruction-level
parallelism in the out-of-order execution engine.

\subsection{Pre-Computed Fixed Huffman Tables}
\label{sec:method:fixedtables}

The DEFLATE fixed Huffman code (block type~1) uses a statically defined code.
Rather than rebuilding the table for each fixed block, we construct it once at
first use and cache it for the lifetime of the process. A 256-byte bit-reversal
lookup table accelerates the canonical-to-reversed code conversion during table
construction.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental}

\subsection{Hardware and Software}
\label{sec:experimental:hardware}

Benchmarks were collected in a containerized x86-64 environment running
Debian~12 with GCC~12.2. Compiler flags were \texttt{-O3 -march=native
-std=c11} for all decoders. PGO builds used GCC's
\texttt{-fprofile-generate}\,/\,\texttt{-fprofile-use} workflow with training on
the full corpus. The container was configured with CPU affinity (\texttt{taskset})
where available.

Reference decoders: system zlib~1.2.x (Debian package), zlib-ng~2.x (built from
source with \texttt{-O3 -march=native}), and libdeflate~1.x (built from source
with default optimization flags).

\label{sec:experimental:corpus}

The benchmark corpus comprises 20 files spanning five categories

\subsection{Measurement Methodology}
\label{sec:experimental:methodology}

Each test case was run for 100 iterations preceded by 5 warmup iterations. We
report the median throughput in MB/s of decompressed output (original size
divided by decode time). Timing uses \texttt{clock\_gettime(CLOCK\_MONOTONIC)}.
Per-file statistics include median, mean, 5th and 95th percentiles, standard
deviation, minimum, and maximum.

Aggregate results use the \emph{geometric mean} across all files at a given
compression level. The geometric mean is preferred over the arithmetic mean
because throughput values span two orders of magnitude (350\,MB/s to
40{,}000\,MB/s for near-incompressible data), preventing high-throughput
outliers from dominating the aggregate.

\subsection{Correctness Validation}
\label{sec:experimental:correctness}

The decoder passes 945 combined test cases: 181 round-trip correctness tests
(comparing byte-for-byte against zlib output for every corpus file at every
compression level and multiple data patterns), 79 fast-decoder-specific
correctness tests, and 685 adversarial test cases (empty streams, stored-only
blocks, maximum back-reference distance, all 286 literal/length codes, maximum
code length of 15~bits, 100+ randomly generated valid DEFLATE streams, and all
boundary conditions). All tests are run under AddressSanitizer and
UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer with zero findings.

Higher compression levels produce denser back-references, slightly reducing
throughput for all decoders. The effect is modest because the additional
compression primarily affects the encoder, not the decoder.

    L1~cache, converting a multi-cycle tree traversal into a single
    $\sim$5-cycle memory access.

  \item \textbf{Packed entries + saved\_bitbuf (v5):} +31.6\% over v3. This
    eliminates four dependent memory accesses (\texttt{length\_base[]},
    \texttt{length\_extra[]}, \texttt{dist\_base[]}, \texttt{dist\_extra[]})
    from the back-reference decode path, replacing them with inline arithmetic
    on the pre-consume bit buffer snapshot. The effect is dramatic because
    back-references constitute 30--60\% of symbols in typical DEFLATE streams.
\end{enumerate}

Multi-literal decode (v2) adds a modest 5.3\%, consistent with the theoretical
analysis: for streams where $\sim$65\% of symbols are literals, decoding up to~3
per refill cycle reduces refill overhead but does not change the per-symbol
critical path length. PGO adds 8.1\% by allowing GCC to optimize branch
layout, function inlining, and register allocation based on actual execution
profiles.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Gap Analysis: Why Zlib-ng and Libdeflate Are Faster}
\label{sec:discussion:gap}

Our decoder reaches 0.80$\times$ of zlib-ng and 0.77$\times$ of libdeflate at
level~6. The remaining gap is attributable to three identifiable factors:

\paragraph{BMI2 BZHI instruction.}
libdeflate and ISA-L use the BMI2 \texttt{BZHI} instruction for branchless
bit-field extraction, replacing a shift-and-mask pair with a single 1-cycle
instruction~\citep{fog2024instrtables}. Our decoder uses portable C
shift-and-mask sequences. While GCC's \texttt{-march=native} generates
\texttt{SHLX}/\texttt{SHRX} for variable shifts, it does not reliably emit
\texttt{BZHI} for our masking patterns. We estimate this accounts for 5--8\%
of the gap to libdeflate.

\paragraph{Match copy efficiency.}
libdeflate uses a carefully tuned \texttt{memcpy}-based overlapping copy that
interacts well with GCC's auto-vectorization. Our explicit SSE2/AVX2 dispatch
introduces branch overhead in the distance-tier selection and may be suboptimal
for the most common short matches (3--10 bytes). On back-reference-heavy files
(english\_prose, text\_1024k), our decoder trails libdeflate by up to 38\%.

\paragraph{Instruction-level parallelism.}
libdeflate achieves better overlap between the bit-reader refill, table lookup,
and match copy through careful instruction ordering. While both decoders
preload the next table entry during match copies, libdeflate's implementation
extracts more ILP from the surrounding code, contributing an estimated 2--4\%
throughput advantage.

\subsection{Per-File-Type Performance Pattern}
\label{sec:discussion:filetype}

The per-file performance gap reveals a structural pattern: our decoder is
weakest on data with high back-reference density and strongest on data with
high literal density or stored blocks.

On binary\_elf (mostly stored blocks, compression ratio 1.1:1), our decoder
achieves 0.98$\times$ of zlib-ng, confirming that the raw block-copy path is
efficient. On english\_prose (compression ratio 5.1:1, $\sim$60\%
back-references), we achieve only 0.78$\times$, indicating that our
back-reference handling is the primary bottleneck.

Small files exhibit disproportionate overhead: text\_1k achieves only
0.36$\times$ of zlib-ng because our 11-bit primary table requires building and
filling 2{,}048 entries, while zlib-ng's 9-bit table requires only 512.
For files where the compressed data is comparable in size to the table,
build cost dominates decode cost.

\subsection{What Did Not Work}
\label{sec:discussion:negative}

Several attempted optimizations produced zero or negative gains:

\paragraph{12-bit primary table.}
Increasing the primary table from 11~bits (8\,KB) to 12~bits (16\,KB) reduced
throughput by 10\% (from 1{,}750 to 1{,}574~MB/s). The additional 8\,KB
causes L1~cache contention with the distance table and output buffer. This
confirms the Hirschberg and Lelewer~\citep{hirschberg1990efficient} analysis
that table size must balance lookup reduction against cache pressure.

\paragraph{4-literal unrolled loop.}
Extending the cascade from 3 to 4 literals caused a 3--8\% regression. The
additional branch reduces the branch predictor's accuracy on the 4th literal
check (near 50/50 literal vs.\ non-literal).

\paragraph{Branchless length-extra extraction.}
Replacing conditional extra-bit reading with branchless arithmetic produced
$\sim$0\% throughput change. The branch predictor handles the extra-bit
conditional with $>$98\% accuracy because the branch direction depends on the
code value, not on data content.

\paragraph{Single-refill back-reference strategy.}
Attempting to decode both length and distance codes from a single refill
produced $\sim$0\% gain. The added complexity of the fast-path check offsets
the saved refill.

\paragraph{Inline short-copy optimization.}
Inlining the match copy for short matches (length $\leq$ 8) with a single
8-byte \texttt{memcpy} caused a 2--5\% regression. Modern CPUs execute the
SSE2 loop body efficiently even for single-iteration cases.

\subsection{The Serial Dependency Ceiling}
\label{sec:discussion:serial}

The fundamental limit on single-threaded DEFLATE decompression is the serial
dependency chain analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:background:serial}. We investigated
multiple approaches to break this chain:

\paragraph{Multi-stream ILP.}
Interleaving two independent Huffman-coded streams within a single block would
enable the CPU's out-of-order engine to overlap their decode chains. However,
this requires a non-standard format (as in FSE/ANS codecs~\citep{collet2015huff0}).

\paragraph{Speculative rollback decoding.}
Starting a speculative decode at a guessed bit position and rolling back on
mismatch. The risk-reward ratio is poor: with codes averaging 7--8 bits, a
random starting position has only a $\sim$12\% chance of converging within
16 symbols, making speculative work wasteful.

\paragraph{Parallel block decoding.}
We investigated parallelizing across DEFLATE blocks using thread-level
parallelism. Empirical analysis of our 20-file corpus compressed at three levels
shows that 50\% of files consist of a single DEFLATE block and 75\% have
$\leq 2$ blocks. Block boundaries require serial parsing, and inter-block LZ77
dependencies prevent independent decode. This finding is consistent with
observations by Knespel et~al.~\citep{knespel2023rapidgzip}, who note that
block-level parallelism requires heuristic sync-point discovery within blocks.

\paragraph{Convergence-based parallel synchronization.}
Starting decoders at arbitrary byte offsets and detecting convergence to the
correct bit alignment, following the approach of
pugz~\citep{pugz2019} for gzip streams. This remains challenging for DEFLATE
because dynamic Huffman tables must be reconstructed at each block boundary.

\subsection{Cross-Architecture Portability}
\label{sec:discussion:crossarch}

We analyzed our decoder's expected performance across six x86-64
microarchitectures using known instruction
latencies~\citep{fog2024instrtables, fog2024microarch}. The core algorithmic
design (11-bit packed tables, 64-bit branchless bitreader, saved-bitbuffer
extraction) is architecture-neutral. Key findings:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{AMD Zen~3/4:} 1-cycle faster L1D latency (4~cycles vs.\
    Intel's 5~cycles) projects 3--5\% higher throughput at equivalent clock
    speeds. PDEP/PEXT performance is not a concern as our decoder avoids these
    instructions entirely.
  \item \textbf{Intel E-cores (Gracemont):} AVX2 instructions execute as
    two 128-bit micro-ops, halving match-copy throughput for distance $\geq 32$.
    Expected throughput: 50--65\% of P-core performance.
  \item \textbf{Apple M1/M3 (projected AArch64 port):} 128\,KB L1D, 3-cycle
    load latency, and 8-wide decode suggest 2.5--4.0\,GB/s, based on
    Johnson's measurements~\citep{johnson2022fasterzlib} and scaling analysis.
\end{itemize}

The only x86-specific code is the SSE2/AVX2 match copy ($\sim$70 lines); all
other optimizations compile to efficient code on any 64-bit architecture.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented a portable C implementation of DEFLATE decompression that,
using six cumulative optimizations, achieves a geometric mean throughput of
2{,}307~MB/s across all compression levels (2{,}414~MB/s at level~6) on
x86-64---a 1.63$\times$ all-level speedup over system zlib (1.75$\times$ at
level~6), rising to 1.89$\times$ with profile-guided optimization.

The ablation study reveals a concentrated optimization landscape: two techniques
account for over 95\% of the total 6.9$\times$ speedup over the naive baseline.
The 11-bit table with 64-bit branchless bitreader provides a 4.9$\times$
improvement by fitting the lookup table in L1~cache and eliminating refill
branches. The packed entry format with saved-bitbuffer extraction adds a further
31.6\% by replacing four dependent memory accesses with inline arithmetic.

The decoder reaches 0.80$\times$ of zlib-ng and 0.77$\times$ of libdeflate.
The remaining gap is attributable to BMI2 instruction usage (5--8\%), match copy
efficiency (5--10\%), and instruction-level parallelism (2--4\%). These are
engineering optimizations; the fundamental limit is the 6--7 cycle serial
dependency per Huffman symbol, which our decoder and all competitors approach
but cannot exceed.

Our investigation confirms three findings relevant to DEFLATE decoder design:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item 11-bit primary tables are empirically optimal for L1-resident decoding
    on current hardware, with 12-bit tables causing measurable regression.
  \item The packed entry format with saved-bitbuffer extraction is the single
    highest-leverage optimization after table lookup itself.
  \item Parallel block decoding of standard DEFLATE streams is not viable for
    typical workloads due to single-block dominance: 50\% of real-world files
    compress to a single block.
\end{enumerate}

Breaking the serial dependency ceiling will require either format modifications
(interleaved entropy streams, pre-computed sync points) or novel speculative
synchronization techniques. The decoder is fully RFC~1951 compliant, passing 945
combined test cases under sanitizer instrumentation, and is suitable for
deployment in latency-sensitive applications where zlib compatibility and
single-threaded throughput are primary requirements.
