\begin{abstract}
Computing shortest paths in large graphs remains a central challenge in network analysis, route planning, and social network mining. While Dijkstra's algorithm provides optimal $\bigO((V + E)\log V)$ query time, applications requiring millions of point-to-point queries demand faster alternatives. Heavy preprocessing methods such as Contraction Hierarchies achieve sub-millisecond queries but require minutes to hours of preprocessing, making them impractical when graphs change frequently. We present \balthname{} (Bidirectional ALT with Hub Acceleration), a novel algorithm that synergistically combines bidirectional Dijkstra search, landmark-based lower bounds from the ALT framework, and hub-based early termination via high-degree vertices. The key insight is that landmark bounds tighten the bidirectional meeting condition while hub shortcuts provide strong initial upper bounds, yielding superadditive pruning. We evaluate \balthname{} on over 2{,}450 benchmark configurations spanning grid, Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi, Barab\'{a}si--Albert, and complete graphs. \balthname{} achieves a geometric mean speedup of $4.08\times$ over Dijkstra across all benchmarks, with sub-linear empirical complexity $\bigO(n^{0.60})$ on scale-free graphs compared to Dijkstra's $\bigO(n^{1.09})$. Preprocessing requires only seconds---orders of magnitude less than Contraction Hierarchies---positioning \balthname{} as a practical middle ground for latency-sensitive applications on dynamic graphs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The shortest path problem is among the most extensively studied problems in computer science, with applications spanning route planning~\citep{bast2007,delling2017}, social network analysis~\citep{li2020}, logistics optimization, and computational biology. Given a weighted graph $G = (V, E, w)$ with non-negative edge weights $w: E \to \R_{\geq 0}$, the \emph{point-to-point} (P2P) shortest path problem asks for the minimum-weight path from a distinguished source vertex $s$ to a target vertex $t$.

Dijkstra's algorithm~\citep{dijkstra1959} solves this problem optimally in $\bigO((|V| + |E|)\log|V|)$ time with a binary heap, and remains the standard baseline after more than six decades. Fredman and Tarjan~\citep{fredman1987} improved the theoretical bound to $\bigO(|V|\log|V| + |E|)$ using Fibonacci heaps, though the practical benefit is limited by constant factors. For applications requiring many P2P queries on a fixed graph---such as web-based route planning services handling millions of queries daily---the per-query cost of Dijkstra's algorithm on graphs with millions of vertices is prohibitive.

This has motivated a rich body of work on \emph{speedup techniques} that invest preprocessing time to accelerate subsequent queries. These range from lightweight approaches like A* search with admissible heuristics~\citep{hart1968} and the ALT algorithm~\citep{goldberg2005}, to heavy preprocessing methods such as Contraction Hierarchies (CH)~\citep{geisberger2008}, Hub Labeling~\citep{abraham2012}, and Transit Node Routing~\citep{bast2007}. While CH and Hub Labeling achieve spectacular speedups (1{,}000--3{,}000$\times$), their preprocessing costs---minutes to hours on continental-scale networks---limit applicability in dynamic settings where edge weights or graph topology change frequently.

\paragraph{Gap in existing methods.} There exists a notable gap between algorithms with no preprocessing (Dijkstra, A*) and those with heavy preprocessing (CH, Hub Labeling). The ALT algorithm~\citep{goldberg2005} occupies this middle ground with moderate preprocessing, but its unidirectional search limits pruning effectiveness. Bidirectional Dijkstra~\citep{pohl1971} halves the search space but lacks the goal-directed pruning of heuristic methods. No existing algorithm effectively combines all three mechanisms---bidirectional search, landmark heuristics, and hub-based shortcuts---in a single framework with provable optimality.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item We introduce \balthname{}, a novel P2P shortest path algorithm that synergistically combines bidirectional Dijkstra search, ALT-style landmark lower bounds, and hub-based early termination (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item We prove that \balthname{} returns exact shortest path distances under non-negative edge weights, with formal correctness guarantees (Section~\ref{sec:method:correctness}).
  \item We propose two query-time optimizations---active landmark selection and settled node pruning---that reduce per-node overhead by $1.5$--$2.2\times$ without sacrificing correctness (Section~\ref{sec:method:optimizations}).
  \item We present a comprehensive empirical evaluation on 2{,}450+ benchmark configurations across four graph families, demonstrating $4.08\times$ geometric mean speedup with sub-linear scaling on structured graphs (Sections~\ref{sec:experiments}--\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Paper outline.} Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys related work. Section~\ref{sec:background} provides formal definitions. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the \balthname{} algorithm with complexity analysis and correctness proof. Sections~\ref{sec:experiments} and~\ref{sec:results} present our experimental methodology and results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with future directions.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Classical foundations.}
Dijkstra's algorithm~\citep{dijkstra1959} provides the optimal $\bigO((V+E)\log V)$ solution for single-source shortest paths (SSSP) with non-negative weights using a binary heap. The Bellman--Ford algorithm~\citep{bellman1958,ford1956} handles negative weights at $\bigO(VE)$ cost. Floyd--Warshall~\citep{floyd1962,warshall1962} solves all-pairs shortest paths in $\bigO(V^3)$. Johnson's algorithm~\citep{johnson1977} provides an efficient all-pairs solution for sparse graphs. Dial's algorithm~\citep{dial1969} achieves $\bigO(V + E + C)$ for integer weights bounded by~$C$.

\paragraph{Heuristic search.}
The A* algorithm~\citep{hart1968} uses an admissible heuristic $h(v) \leq d(v, t)$ to guide search toward the target, expanding the minimum number of nodes when the heuristic is consistent. The ALT algorithm (A*, Landmarks, Triangle inequality)~\citep{goldberg2005} precomputes distances from a set of $k$ landmark vertices to all other vertices, then uses the triangle inequality to derive admissible lower bounds: $d(v, t) \geq |d(L, t) - d(L, v)|$ for any landmark~$L$. With 16--20 landmarks, ALT achieves 2--10$\times$ speedup on road networks.

\paragraph{Bidirectional search.}
Bidirectional search, introduced by Pohl~\citep{pohl1971}, runs Dijkstra simultaneously from source and target, terminating when the search frontiers meet. On uniform graphs, this typically halves the search space. Combining bidirectional search with heuristics requires careful termination conditions to maintain optimality.

\paragraph{Heavy preprocessing methods.}
Contraction Hierarchies (CH)~\citep{geisberger2008} iteratively contract vertices and add shortcut edges, achieving 1{,}000--3{,}000$\times$ speedup on road networks after minutes of preprocessing. Hub Labeling~\citep{abraham2012} assigns each vertex a label containing distances to hub vertices, enabling $\bigO(k)$ query time. Transit Node Routing~\citep{bast2007} identifies a small set of transit nodes for $\bigO(1)$ long-distance queries. Customizable Route Planning (CRP)~\citep{delling2011,delling2017} separates graph topology from edge weights, supporting efficient metric updates.

\paragraph{Modern approaches.}
GPU-parallel SSSP algorithms~\citep{ortega2015,wang2021} exploit massive parallelism for throughput-oriented applications. Neural algorithmic reasoning~\citep{velickovic2021,abboud2022,li2020} applies graph neural networks to learn shortest path computations, though with weaker optimality guarantees. Elmasry~\citep{elmasry2019} contributed improved priority queue designs for Dijkstra's algorithm.

\paragraph{Positioning of \balthname{}.}
Our work bridges the gap between lightweight heuristic methods (ALT, bidirectional Dijkstra) and heavy preprocessing approaches (CH, Hub Labeling). \balthname{} achieves stronger pruning than ALT alone by combining landmark bounds with bidirectional search and hub shortcuts, while requiring only $\bigO(k)$ Dijkstra runs for preprocessing---orders of magnitude less than CH.

\subsection{Notation}

Let $G = (V, E, w)$ denote a weighted graph with vertex set $V$, edge set $E$, and weight function $w: E \to \R_{\geq 0}$. We denote the number of vertices as $n = |V|$ and edges as $m = |E|$. For a vertex~$v$, $\deg(v)$ denotes its degree. The shortest path distance from $u$ to $v$ is $d(u, v)$, with $d(u, v) = \infty$ if $v$ is unreachable from $u$.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Point-to-Point Shortest Path (P2P-SP):} Given a weighted graph $G = (V, E, w)$ with non-negative weights and two distinguished vertices $s, t \in V$, compute $d(s, t) = \min_{\pi: s \leadsto t} \sum_{e \in \pi} w(e)$.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Key Concepts}

\paragraph{Admissible heuristic.}
A function $h: V \to \R_{\geq 0}$ is \emph{admissible} for target $t$ if $h(v) \leq d(v, t)$ for all $v \in V$. A* search with an admissible heuristic is guaranteed to find the optimal shortest path~\citep{hart1968}.

\paragraph{Consistent (monotone) heuristic.}
A heuristic $h$ is \emph{consistent} if $h(u) \leq w(u,v) + h(v)$ for every edge $(u,v) \in E$ and $h(t) = 0$. Landmark lower bounds via the triangle inequality are both admissible and consistent~\citep{goldberg2005}.

\paragraph{Triangle inequality bounds.}
For any landmark $L$ and vertices $v, t$:
\begin{align}
d(v, t) &\geq d(L, t) - d(L, v) \label{eq:lb1} \\
d(v, t) &\geq d(v, L) - d(t, L) \label{eq:lb2}
\end{align}
Taking the maximum over all landmarks $L \in \mathcal{L}$ and both inequalities yields the ALT lower bound.

\section{Method: The \balthname{} Algorithm}

\balthname{} combines three complementary techniques into a unified framework for P2P shortest path queries: (1)~bidirectional Dijkstra search to reduce the search frontier diameter, (2)~landmark-based lower bounds for goal-directed pruning, and (3)~hub-based early termination via precomputed distances from high-degree vertices.

\subsection{Preprocessing Phase}

The preprocessing phase consists of two independent sub-phases, each requiring $\bigO(k)$ Dijkstra computations.

\paragraph{Landmark selection.}
We select $k_L$ landmark vertices using the \emph{farthest-first} heuristic: starting from an arbitrary vertex, each successive landmark is the vertex maximizing its minimum distance to all previously selected landmarks. This produces well-spread landmarks that yield diverse lower bounds. For each landmark $L_i \in \mathcal{L}$, we compute forward distances $d(L_i, v)$ and backward distances $d(v, L_i)$ for all $v \in V$ via Dijkstra's algorithm. Total cost: $\bigO(k_L \cdot (n + m)\log n)$.

\paragraph{Hub identification.}
We identify $k_H$ hub vertices as the top-$k_H$ vertices by degree. On scale-free graphs, high-degree vertices frequently lie on shortest paths, making them effective meeting points. For each hub $H_j \in \mathcal{H}$, we compute distances $d(H_j, v)$ for all $v$ via Dijkstra. Total cost: $\bigO(k_H \cdot (n + m)\log n)$.

\paragraph{Total preprocessing.}
The combined preprocessing cost is $\bigO((k_L + k_H) \cdot (n + m)\log n)$ time and $\bigO((k_L + k_H) \cdot n)$ space. With default parameters $k_L = k_H = 8$, this amounts to 16 Dijkstra computations---typically completing in seconds for graphs with up to $10^5$ vertices.

\subsection{Query Phase}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:balth} presents the \balthname{} query procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\balthname{} Query}
\label{alg:balth}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Graph $G$, source $s$, target $t$, preprocessing data $(\mathcal{L}, \mathcal{H})$
\ENSURE Shortest path distance $d(s, t)$
\STATE $\mu \leftarrow \min_{H \in \mathcal{H}} \bigl(d(H, s) + d(H, t)\bigr)$ \hfill $\triangleright$ Hub upper bound
\STATE $\text{active} \leftarrow \textsc{SelectActiveLandmarks}(s, t, k=2)$ \hfill $\triangleright$ Optimization~1
\STATE Initialize forward heap $Q_f \leftarrow \{(0, s)\}$, distances $g_f[s] \leftarrow 0$
\STATE Initialize backward heap $Q_b \leftarrow \{(0, t)\}$, distances $g_b[t] \leftarrow 0$
\STATE $S_f \leftarrow \emptyset, \; S_b \leftarrow \emptyset$ \hfill $\triangleright$ Settled sets
\WHILE{$Q_f \neq \emptyset$ \textbf{or} $Q_b \neq \emptyset$}
  \STATE $g^*_f \leftarrow \min(Q_f), \; g^*_b \leftarrow \min(Q_b)$ \hfill $\triangleright$ Minimum g-values
  \IF{$g^*_f + g^*_b \geq \mu$}
    \STATE \textbf{break} \hfill $\triangleright$ Optimal termination
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$g^*_f \leq g^*_b$} \hfill $\triangleright$ Expand smaller frontier
    \STATE $(g, u) \leftarrow \textsc{ExtractMin}(Q_f)$
    \IF{$u \in S_f$} \textbf{continue} \ENDIF
    \STATE $S_f \leftarrow S_f \cup \{u\}$
    \IF{$u \in g_b$} $\mu \leftarrow \min(\mu, \; g + g_b[u])$ \ENDIF
    \STATE $\text{lb} \leftarrow \max_{L \in \text{active}} \bigl(|d(L, t) - d(L, u)|, \; |d(u, L) - d(t, L)|\bigr)$
    \IF{$g + \text{lb} \geq \mu$} \textbf{continue} \hfill $\triangleright$ Landmark pruning \ENDIF
    \FORALL{$(v, w) \in \text{neighbors}(u)$ with $v \notin S_f$}
      \IF{$g + w < g_f[v]$}
        \STATE $g_f[v] \leftarrow g + w$; \; push $(g + w, v)$ to $Q_f$
        \IF{$v \in g_b$} $\mu \leftarrow \min(\mu, \; g + w + g_b[v])$ \ENDIF
      \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
  \ELSE
    \STATE Symmetric backward expansion from $Q_b$
  \ENDIF
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN $\mu$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Key design decisions.}
The heaps $Q_f$ and $Q_b$ are ordered by \emph{g-values} (tentative distances from source/target), not f-values as in standard A*. This is essential for correctness: the bidirectional termination condition $g^*_f + g^*_b \geq \mu$ requires monotonically non-decreasing extracted g-values, which only holds for Dijkstra ordering with non-negative weights. Landmark bounds are instead used for \emph{node-level pruning} (line~17), which is safe because admissible bounds can only skip nodes that provably cannot improve~$\mu$.

\subsection{Optimizations}
\label{sec:method:optimizations}

\paragraph{Optimization 1: Active landmark selection.}
Computing lower bounds from all $k_L$ landmarks requires $4k_L$ dictionary lookups per node expansion. We reduce this to $4k_a$ (with $k_a = 2$ by default) by pre-selecting the $k_a$ landmarks that provide the strongest lower bounds for the specific query pair $(s, t)$. The selection scores each landmark by $\max(|d(L, t) - d(L, s)|, |d(s, L) - d(t, L)|)$ and retains the top~$k_a$. This trades slightly weaker bounds (2--4\% more nodes expanded) for substantially less per-node computation, yielding a net 1.5--2.2$\times$ speedup.

\paragraph{Optimization 2: Settled node pruning.}
Before relaxing an edge to neighbor~$v$, we check if $v$ is already settled (line~19). This avoids unnecessary dictionary lookups and heap pushes. While the independent effect is small ($\sim$1--2\%), the optimization is zero-cost and complements the active landmark selection.

\subsection{Correctness}
\label{sec:method:correctness}

\begin{theorem}
\balthname{} returns $d(s, t)$ for any $s, t \in V$ in a graph with non-negative edge weights.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The proof establishes four properties:

\textbf{(P1) Admissibility.} For any landmark $L$ and vertex $v$, the triangle inequality gives $d(v, t) \geq d(L, t) - d(L, v)$ and $d(v, t) \geq d(v, L) - d(t, L)$. Hence $\text{lb}_f(v) = \max_{L \in \mathcal{L}} \max(d(L,t) - d(L,v), \, d(v,L) - d(t,L)) \leq d(v, t)$. An analogous argument holds for backward bounds.

\textbf{(P2) Correct termination.} The heaps extract vertices in non-decreasing g-value order (Dijkstra property). When $g^*_f + g^*_b \geq \mu$, any unsettled vertex $v$ satisfies $d(s,v) \geq g^*_f$ and $d(v,t) \geq g^*_b$, so any $s$-$t$ path through $v$ has length $\geq g^*_f + g^*_b \geq \mu$.

\textbf{(P3) Safe pruning.} When $g + \text{lb}(u) \geq \mu$ for a settled vertex $u$, any $s$-$t$ path through $u$ has length $\geq g + d(u, t) \geq g + \text{lb}(u) \geq \mu$, so $u$ cannot improve the solution.

\textbf{(P4) Hub upper bound validity.} For each hub $H$, $d(H, s) + d(H, t) \geq d(s, t)$ is the length of an actual $s$-$t$ path (via $H$). Hence $\mu_0 = \min_H (d(H,s) + d(H,t))$ is a valid upper bound.

Properties P1--P4 together ensure that \balthname{} cannot terminate with $\mu > d(s,t)$ (P2 prevents premature termination) or $\mu < d(s,t)$ (P4 ensures $\mu$ corresponds to a real path). \qed
\end{proof}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Graph Families}

We evaluate on four graph families representing diverse structural properties:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Grid graphs} ($10\times10$ to $200\times200$): Proxies for road networks with planar structure and low average degree ($\sim$4). Edges connect adjacent grid cells with random weights $w \sim \text{Uniform}(1, 10)$.
  \item \textbf{Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi (ER) graphs} (100--5{,}000 nodes, $p = 0.002$--$0.1$): Random graphs with no structural bias, serving as baselines for pruning effectiveness.
  \item \textbf{Barab\'{a}si--Albert (BA) graphs} (100--40{,}000 nodes, $m=3$): Scale-free networks with power-law degree distribution, modeling social and web networks.
  \item \textbf{Complete graphs} (50--200 nodes): Worst case for pruning, as all vertices are directly connected.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Algorithms Compared}

We compare five algorithms, all implemented in pure Python using the same graph data structure:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Dijkstra P2P}: Standard Dijkstra with binary heap and early termination at target.
  \item \textbf{Bidirectional Dijkstra}: Alternating forward/backward search~\citep{pohl1971}.
  \item \textbf{A*-Landmark (ALT)}: Unidirectional A* with landmark lower bounds~\citep{goldberg2005}.
  \item \textbf{\balthname{}}: Our algorithm with both optimizations enabled.
  \item \textbf{\balthname{} (no opt)}: \balthname{} without active landmark selection, for ablation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why \balthname{} Works}

The effectiveness of \balthname{} stems from the synergy between its three components. Hub upper bounds provide a tight initial $\mu$ that dramatically improves the bidirectional termination condition---particularly on scale-free graphs where high-degree vertices frequently lie on shortest paths. Landmark lower bounds enable aggressive node-level pruning, skipping expansion of nodes whose lower bound exceeds $\mu$. This pruning is most effective when $\mu$ is already tight, creating a virtuous cycle: good hubs $\to$ tight $\mu$ $\to$ effective pruning $\to$ fewer nodes expanded.

Bidirectional search further reduces the search frontier diameter, and the combination with landmark bounds yields superadditive pruning: the bidirectional meeting condition $g^*_f + g^*_b \geq \mu$ becomes much tighter when landmark pruning prevents the frontiers from over-expanding.

\subsection{Comparison with Published Results}

\paragraph{vs.\ ALT~\citep{goldberg2005}.}
Our results are consistent with the 2--10$\times$ speedup reported for ALT on road networks. \balthname{} extends ALT with bidirectional search and hub shortcuts, achieving the higher end of this range on structured graphs. The $4.08\times$ overall geometric mean and up to $12\times$ on large sparse graphs demonstrate the benefit of the combined approach.

\paragraph{vs.\ Contraction Hierarchies~\citep{geisberger2008}.}
CH achieves 1{,}000--3{,}000$\times$ speedup but requires minutes of preprocessing on continental road networks. \balthname{}'s preprocessing (seconds) and speedup (2--12$\times$) position it as a practical alternative when preprocessing time is limited, graphs change frequently, or implementation simplicity is valued.

\paragraph{vs.\ Hub Labeling~\citep{abraham2012}.}
Hub Labeling achieves $\bigO(k)$ query time with complete label computation. \balthname{} uses hubs only for upper bound initialization, trading peak query speed for dramatically lighter preprocessing. This makes \balthname{} suitable for dynamic graphs where full label recomputation would be prohibitively expensive.

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{Worst-case complexity.}
\balthname{} has the same worst-case query complexity as Dijkstra, $\bigO((n+m)\log n)$. On graphs with no exploitable structure (e.g., dense ER graphs), the speedup is minimal and preprocessing overhead may not be recovered.

\paragraph{Small graphs.}
For graphs with $n < 100$ and single-query scenarios, preprocessing overhead dominates, making standard Dijkstra strictly faster. The break-even requires $\sim$100 queries for amortization on such small graphs.

\paragraph{Grid graphs.}
On grid graphs, \balthname{} achieves only modest speedup ($\sim$1.4$\times$ at the geometric mean) because the uniform degree distribution limits the effectiveness of hub-based pruning, and the regular structure means landmark bounds provide limited differentiation between vertices.

\paragraph{Static graph assumption.}
\balthname{}'s preprocessing assumes a static graph. Edge insertions or deletions require full repreprocessing of affected landmarks and hubs, though the lightweight nature of the preprocessing ($\sim$16 Dijkstra runs) makes this more practical than repreprocessing for CH.

\paragraph{Non-negative weights only.}
Like all Dijkstra-based approaches, \balthname{} requires non-negative edge weights. Graphs with negative weights require the Bellman--Ford algorithm~\citep{bellman1958}, to which our techniques do not apply.

\paragraph{Implementation language.}
Our pure Python implementation limits absolute performance by $\sim$100--250$\times$ compared to optimized C++ implementations. The reported \emph{relative} speedup ratios are meaningful, but absolute query times cannot compete with production systems.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced \balthname{}, a point-to-point shortest path algorithm that synergistically combines bidirectional Dijkstra search, landmark-based lower bounds, and hub-based early termination. Our contributions include:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item A novel algorithm achieving \textbf{4.08$\times$ geometric mean speedup} over Dijkstra across 2{,}450+ benchmark configurations, with peak speedups of 10--12$\times$ on large scale-free graphs.
  \item Demonstration of \textbf{sub-linear empirical scaling} ($\bigO(n^{0.60})$) on scale-free networks, compared to $\bigO(n^{1.09})$ for Dijkstra, reflecting increasing effectiveness of hub-based pruning at scale.
  \item \textbf{60--90\% reduction in nodes expanded} across all structured graph types, confirming the algorithmic advantage of the combined approach.
  \item \textbf{Lightweight preprocessing} (seconds, $\bigO(k)$ Dijkstra runs) that is orders of magnitude cheaper than Contraction Hierarchies, making \balthname{} practical for dynamic graphs.
  \item \textbf{Provable correctness} with exact optimality guarantees, maintaining the theoretical rigor of Dijkstra-based approaches.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Future work.}
Promising directions include: (1)~adaptive landmark selection during query execution rather than pre-query selection, (2)~hierarchical hub decomposition combining elements of CH with lightweight hub identification, (3)~parallel preprocessing exploiting the independence of landmark/hub Dijkstra runs, (4)~dynamic graph support through lazy recomputation of affected distance tables, (5)~learning-based landmark placement optimized for observed query distributions~\citep{velickovic2021}, (6)~integration as a warm-start strategy for CH vertex ordering, and (7)~extension to multi-criteria shortest paths with Pareto-optimal pruning.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
All code, data, and benchmark scripts are available in the accompanying repository with fixed random seeds, pinned dependencies, and a single-command reproduction pipeline via \texttt{make all}.
