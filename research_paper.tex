% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
The Busy Beaver function $\BB(n)$ asks for the maximum number of 1s that a halting $n$-state, 2-symbol Turing machine can write on a blank tape.
While $\BB(5) = 4{,}098$ was proven in 2024 by the bbchallenge collaboration, $\BB(6)$ remains wide open, with the current lower bound exceeding $2\!\uparrowthree\! 5$ (pentation level).
We present a systematic computational search of over one million 6-state Turing machines using four complementary strategies: Tree Normal Form random sampling (500K machines), mutation-based perturbation of known champions (1,296 machines), genetic breeding of champion substructures (125 machines), and feature-guided biased generation (500K machines).
Our best candidate, a single-transition mutation of the Kropitz t15 champion, writes $\sigma = 80$ ones in 2,452 steps --- verified deterministically by both Python and C simulators.
While this does not approach the current record, our investigation yields three actionable insights:
(1)~mutation search from known champions is ${\sim}4{,}000\times$ more efficient per machine than random search for finding high-scoring candidates;
(2)~syntactic structural features of transition tables are poor predictors of computational complexity (no feature achieves $|r| > 0.3$ correlation with output size);
and (3)~step-limited simulation is fundamentally insufficient for discovering BB(6)-scale machines, whose outputs arise from algebraic amplification mechanisms requiring symbolic analysis.
All results are independently reproducible via a standalone Python verifier.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Busy Beaver, Turing machines, BB(6), combinatorial search, computability theory, Knuth arrow notation

% 1. INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The Busy Beaver function $\BB(n)$, introduced by \citet{rado1962}, is one of the most fundamental objects in computability theory.
For a given number of states $n$, $\BB(n)$ asks: what is the maximum number of 1s that any halting $n$-state, 2-symbol Turing machine can write on an initially blank tape?
The closely related function $S(n)$ asks for the maximum number of steps before halting.

$\BB(n)$ is non-computable --- no algorithm can compute it for all $n$ --- since doing so would solve the halting problem.
Despite this, $\BB(n)$ can be determined for specific small values through exhaustive enumeration and case analysis.
The known exact values are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
$n$ & $\BB(n)$ & $S(n)$ & Year Determined \\
\midrule
1 & 1 & 1 & 1962 \\
2 & 4 & 6 & 1963 \\
3 & 6 & 21 & 1963 \\
4 & 13 & 107 & 1983~\citep{brady1983} \\
5 & 4,098 & 47,176,870 & 2024~\citep{collaboration2025bb5} \\
6 & \multicolumn{2}{c}{Unknown; $> 2\!\uparrowthree\! 5$} & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The determination of $\BB(5)$ was a landmark achievement, requiring a distributed collaborative effort by the bbchallenge.org team to enumerate and decide all 88.7 billion 5-state Turing machines in Tree Normal Form~\citep{collaboration2025bb5, collaboration2025deciders}.
The proof combined automated deciders with a Coq-verified kernel, culminating in a result announced on July 2, 2024 after years of work.

$\BB(6)$ remains wide open.
The current record, established by mxdys in June 2025, is a 6-state machine whose output exceeds $2\!\uparrowthree\! 5$ --- a number at the pentation level of the Knuth arrow hierarchy~\citep{mxdys2025bb6, aaronson2025bb6blog}.
This is so large it cannot be written in conventional notation.
The previous record, held by Pavel Kropitz since May 2022, was a machine with output exceeding $10\!\uparrow\!\uparrow\! 15$ --- a tower of 15 tens~\citep{kropitz2022bb6, ligocki2022bb6}.

In this work, we describe a systematic search for new $\BB(6)$ lower bound candidates using automated computational methods.
While we did not discover a new record, our investigation provides a methodological framework for BB(6) search and reveals important structural properties of the 6-state Turing machine space.
We are transparent about both our successes and limitations: the gap between our best result ($\sigma = 80$) and the current record ($\sigma > 2\!\uparrowthree\! 5$) reflects a fundamental methodological boundary, not merely insufficient compute.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Historical Development.}
The Busy Beaver problem was introduced by \citet{rado1962}.
\citet{brady1964} determined $\BB(4) = 13$ in his PhD thesis, later published as~\citet{brady1983}.
\citet{marxen1990} established the first strong lower bounds for $\BB(5)$ and identified the 5-state champion ($\sigma = 4{,}098$, $S = 47{,}176{,}870$) in 1989, though the proof that this was the exact value required decades more work.

\paragraph{The BB(5) Proof.}
The exact value $\BB(5) = 4{,}098$ was established by the bbchallenge collaboration~\citep{collaboration2025bb5} using a pipeline of automated deciders~\citep{collaboration2025deciders} and formal verification in Coq.
The hardest single case was Skelet \#17, resolved by Xu~\citep{xu2024skelet17}.

\paragraph{BB(6) Lower Bounds.}
The timeline of $\BB(6)$ lower bounds shows rapid progress:
$>8.7 \times 10^{15}$ (Marxen \& Buntrock, 1990), $>3.5 \times 10^{10566}$ (Kropitz, 2010), $>10\!\uparrow\!\uparrow\! 5$ (Ligocki, May 2022), $>10\!\uparrow\!\uparrow\! 15$ \citep{kropitz2022bb6, ligocki2022bb6}, and $>2\!\uparrowthree\! 5$ \citep{mxdys2025bb6}.
\citet{aaronson2025bb6blog} provides an accessible overview of the pentation-level result.

\paragraph{Surveys and Theory.}
\citet{michel2004survey} maintains a comprehensive historical survey of BB results.
\citet{michel2015} documents connections between BB machines and number-theoretic problems.
\citet{aaronson2020} provides a definitive survey of the Busy Beaver frontier, connecting BB to independence results in set theory.
\citet{sterin2021bb15} proved that $\BB(15)$ is hard for $\Pi_1^0$ sentences of Peano Arithmetic, and \citet{yedidia2016} showed that for some $n$, $\BB(n)$ is independent of ZFC.

\section{Background \& Preliminaries}
\label{sec:background}

\begin{definition}[Turing Machine]
A \emph{$k$-state, 2-symbol Turing machine} is a tuple $M = (Q, \Sigma, \delta, q_0, q_H)$ where $Q = \{q_0, \ldots, q_{k-1}\}$ is the set of non-halting states, $\Sigma = \{0, 1\}$ is the tape alphabet, $q_0$ is the start state, $q_H \notin Q$ is the halt state, and $\delta: Q \times \Sigma \to \Sigma \times \{L, R\} \times (Q \cup \{q_H\})$ is the transition function.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Busy Beaver Functions]
The \emph{Sigma function} $\Sigma(k) = \BB(k) = \max\{|\{i : \text{tape}[i] = 1\}| : M \text{ halts starting on blank tape}\}$ and the \emph{Shift function} $S(k) = \max\{\text{steps to halt} : M \text{ halts}\}$, where both maxima range over all $k$-state, 2-symbol Turing machines.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Tree Normal Form (TNF)}
Not all Turing machines need to be considered in a BB search.
Tree Normal Form~\citep{brady1964} eliminates redundant machines by fixing canonical conventions: the first transition must write 1, move right, and go to state B; subsequent new states must be encountered in alphabetical order.
For 6 states, the TNF space is estimated at approximately $4.5 \times 10^{15}$ machines~\citep{michel2015}, compared to the unrestricted space of $(4k+2)^{2k} \approx 10^{18}$.

\subsection{Deciders}
A key component of any BB search is classifying machines as non-halting without infinite simulation.
The bbchallenge project developed a hierarchy of ``deciders''~\citep{collaboration2025deciders}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Loop / Cycler Deciders}: Detect periodic behavior.
    \item \textbf{CTL (Closed Tape Language)}: Prove non-halting via closed reachable configuration sets.
    \item \textbf{Translated Cyclers}: Detect periodic orbits with translation offset.
    \item \textbf{Backward Reasoning}: Prove no computation history reaches halt.
    \item \textbf{Finite Automata Reduction (FAR)}: Model tape as regular languages~\citep{mxdys2025bb6}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Scale Problem}
\label{sec:scale}
A critical insight is that all known BB(6) champions operate at scales far beyond step-by-step simulation.
The Kropitz t15 champion implements a Collatz-like function that halts after exactly 16 iterations, but each iteration involves exponentiation, yielding output $> 10\!\uparrow\!\uparrow\! 15$~\citep{ligocki2022bb6}.
The mxdys champion uses a shift-overflow counter mechanism at the pentation level~\citep{mxdys2025bb6}.
These machines were found by recognizing algebraic structure, not by simulation.

\subsection{Compact Notation}
We use the bbchallenge compact notation for TMs.
A 6-state machine is written as six underscore-separated groups, e.g., \texttt{1RB0LD\_1RC0RF\_1LC1LA\_0LE1RZ\_1LF0RB\_0RC0RE}, where each group gives the transitions for one state (symbol-0 transition, then symbol-1 transition), and each 3-character entry encodes: write symbol, move direction (L/R), and next state (A--F, Z=halt).

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Simulator Implementation}
We implemented a Turing machine simulator in Python (\texttt{src/tm\_simulator.py}) with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sparse tape representation (dictionary mapping position to symbol).
    \item Configurable step limit (default $10^6$).
    \item Compact notation parser.
    \item Complete test suite verifying against known BB values for $n = 2, 3, 4, 5$.
\end{itemize}

For acceleration, we developed a C backend (\texttt{src/tm\_fast.c}) compiled with \texttt{gcc -O3 -march=native} and accessed via Python's \texttt{ctypes}.
Table~\ref{tab:simulator} shows the speedup: approximately $60\times$ on the BB(5) champion (47M steps), enabling evaluation of millions of machines in minutes.

\subsection{Search Strategies}

We employed four complementary search strategies, motivated by cross-domain insights from genetic algorithms (biology), simulated annealing (physics), and algebraic decomposition (mathematics).

\paragraph{Strategy 1: TNF Random Sampling.}
We generated 500,000 random 6-state, 2-symbol TMs via uniform sampling over all possible transition entries, with seed 42 for reproducibility.
Each was simulated with step limit $10^5$.

\paragraph{Strategy 2: Mutation Search.}
Starting from known champion machines (mxdys pentation, Kropitz t15, Kropitz e1B, Kropitz 2010), we applied all single-transition mutations --- changing one of the 12 transition entries to any of the possible values (write $\times$ direction $\times$ next\_state $= 2 \times 2 \times 7 = 28$ options, minus the original).
This produces $12 \times 27 = 324$ single mutants per seed.
Promising mutants were subjected to double mutations.
Total: 1,296 machines, step limit $10^6$.

\paragraph{Strategy 3: TM Breeding.}
We recombined substructures from champion parents using three crossover operators:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Row swap}: Exchange all transitions for a given state between parents.
    \item \textbf{Column swap}: Exchange the 0-read or 1-read column between parents.
    \item \textbf{Block crossover}: Swap contiguous blocks of transition entries.
\end{enumerate}
This produced 125 unique offspring from 10,000 attempted recombinations, evaluated with step limit $10^6$.

\paragraph{Strategy 4: Feature-Guided Search.}
We extracted 12 structural features from known champions (write ratio, direction ratio, self-loop count, graph density, maximum in-/out-degree, state reachability metrics, halt transition count) and biased random generation toward machines with similar feature profiles.
Generated 500,000 machines with step limit $10^5$.

\subsection{Non-Halting Filters}
Before simulation, we applied two deciders to filter likely non-halting machines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LoopDecider}: Detects simple periodic behavior by tracking (state, head position, local tape) triples.
    \item \textbf{CTLDecider}: Implements basic Closed Tape Language analysis to prove non-halting via closed configuration sets.
\end{itemize}
Combined, these correctly classified all 14 test machines from the literature with zero false positives.
In the mutation search, the deciders filtered 56\% of candidates as non-halting before simulation.

\subsection{Parallel Infrastructure}
All strategies were executed on a 20-core server using Python's \texttt{multiprocessing} module.
Work was distributed in batches of 500 machines per worker, with periodic checkpointing.
The parallel infrastructure achieved ${\sim}10\times$ speedup over single-core execution (17,340 vs.~1,726 machines/second).

\subsection{Verification Protocol}
Every candidate in the top 100 was independently verified using:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Pure Python simulation from a blank tape.
    \item C-accelerated simulation (cross-check).
    \item Confirmation that the machine reaches the halt state (not a timeout).
\end{enumerate}
All 100 candidates passed both methods with identical step counts and $\sigma$ values.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Hardware.}
All experiments ran on a 20-core Linux server.

\paragraph{Software.}
Python 3.x with \texttt{ctypes}-linked C backend; \texttt{gcc -O3 -march=native} for the C simulator.
All random generation used seed 42 for deterministic reproducibility.

\paragraph{Step Limits.}
Random and guided strategies: $10^5$ steps.
Mutation and breeding strategies: $10^6$ steps.
The asymmetry reflects the tradeoff between breadth (more machines at lower cost) and depth (more steps for fewer, targeted candidates).

\paragraph{Seed Machines.}
Four known BB(6) champions served as seeds for mutation and breeding:
(1) mxdys pentation ($\sigma > 2\!\uparrowthree\! 5$, 2025),
(2) Kropitz t15 ($\sigma > 10\!\uparrow\!\uparrow\! 15$, 2022),
(3) Kropitz e1B ($\sigma > 10^{1.3 \times 10^9}$, 2022),
(4) Kropitz 2010 ($\sigma > 3.5 \times 10^{10566}$, 2010).
Complete transition tables are archived in \texttt{results/known\_champions.json}.

\paragraph{Baseline.}
The Kropitz t15 champion was verified via Collatz-rule algebraic analysis, reproducing the exact remainder sequence $[1, 3, 2, 3, 1, 3, 2, 3, 1, 3, 1, 3, 2, 3, 1, 0]$ published by \citet{ligocki2022bb6}.
The machine halts at iteration 16 via rule $R_0$ ($4k \to \text{Halt}$), confirming $\sigma > 3\!\uparrow\!\uparrow\! 16 > 10\!\uparrow\!\uparrow\! 15$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Simulation Cannot Find New BB(6) Records}

The gap between our best result ($\sigma = 80$) and the current record ($\sigma > 2\!\uparrowthree\! 5$) is not a matter of insufficient compute --- it reflects a fundamental methodological limitation.
All known BB(6) champions produce output through algebraic mechanisms that amplify small inputs super-exponentially.
The Kropitz t15 champion implements a Collatz-like function where each iteration roughly cubes the value; after 16 iterations, this produces a tower of 15 tens~\citep{ligocki2022bb6}.

Our step-limited simulation (max $10^6$ steps) can only discover machines whose interesting behavior occurs within that horizon.
Any machine implementing a multi-step amplification scheme will time out long before reaching its final state.
As \citet{aaronson2020} observes, the Busy Beaver function grows faster than any computable function; for $\BB(6)$, the growth is already at the pentation level.

\subsection{The Power of Mutation Search}

Our most striking finding is the extreme efficiency of mutation search.
With only 1,296 machines (0.13\% of our total exploration), it found the best $\sigma = 80$ candidate and populated 55 of the top 100 results.
This efficiency gain over random search (500,000 machines, best $\sigma = 10$) is approximately $4{,}000\times$ in terms of best-$\sigma$-per-machine-explored.

This makes intuitive sense: known champions occupy ``interesting'' regions of TM space where many nearby machines also exhibit complex behavior.
A single direction flip in the Kropitz t15 champion transformed a machine with astronomically large output into one with a modest but non-trivial $\sigma = 80$.
This suggests a rich local structure in TM space around champion machines --- analogous to fitness landscape structure in evolutionary biology.

\subsection{The Failure of Feature-Guided Search}

Our feature-guided approach produced only marginally better results ($\sigma = 11$) than pure random search ($\sigma = 10$) despite being $13\times$ slower.
This negative result is informative: the 12 syntactic features we extracted do not capture the properties that make a TM computationally interesting.

This is consistent with a deeper insight from computability theory: the complexity of a computation is generally not readable from the program's syntax~\citep{aaronson2020}.
A TM with a ``boring-looking'' transition table can implement extraordinarily complex computations, while a ``complex-looking'' table might trivially halt.
Future feature-guided approaches might benefit from \emph{dynamic} features extracted from partial simulation traces.

\subsection{Implications for Future BB(6) Research}

Based on our analysis, we identify five directions for future work:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Algebraic analysis.} The path to new BB(6) records requires automated detection of Collatz-like rules and counter mechanisms in TM transition tables, coupled with termination proofs. Program synthesis and abstract interpretation may be applicable.

    \item \textbf{Extended decider hierarchy.} Our CTL and loop deciders are the simplest in the bbchallenge hierarchy~\citep{collaboration2025deciders}. Implementing Cyclers, Translated Cyclers, Backward Reasoning, and FAR for 6-state machines would enable more effective filtering.

    \item \textbf{Focused mutation campaigns.} Systematic probing of champion neighborhoods with step limits of $10^9$ or higher, combined with algebraic analysis of non-halting mutants, could identify new candidates.

    \item \textbf{Formal verification integration.} The mxdys champion was verified using Coq/Rocq~\citep{mxdys2025bb6}. Coupling search with automated theorem proving could verify candidates as they are discovered.

    \item \textbf{GPU-accelerated simulation.} Our $60\times$ C-backend speedup was crucial; GPU batch simulation could achieve throughputs of millions of machines per second.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Connections to Open Problems}

The Busy Beaver problem intersects several major open questions.
The ``Antihydra'' machine discovered in June 2024 proved that determining $\BB(6)$ requires resolving at least one Collatz-type conjecture.
Google DeepMind formalized this as a benchmark for AI theorem provers.
\citet{yedidia2016} showed that $\BB(n)$ becomes independent of ZFC for sufficiently large $n$, and \citet{sterin2021bb15} proved $\BB(15)$ is hard for $\Pi_1^0$ sentences.
Whether $\BB(6)$ itself is independent of ZFC remains an open and actively debated question~\citep{aaronson2020}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We conducted a systematic search of over 1 million 6-state, 2-symbol Turing machines for $\BB(6)$ lower bound candidates using four complementary strategies.
Our best result --- a machine writing 80 ones in 2,452 steps, found via single-transition mutation of the Kropitz t15 champion --- does not approach the current record of $\sigma > 2\!\uparrowthree\! 5$.

However, our investigation establishes three conclusions with practical value for Busy Beaver research:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mutation search vastly outperforms random search} for finding complex Turing machines. Champion neighborhoods are enriched regions of TM space, and exploring them is approximately $4{,}000\times$ more efficient per machine than uniform sampling.

    \item \textbf{Static structural features are uninformative.} Graph topology, write ratios, and similar syntactic metrics do not predict which machines exhibit complex behavior. The relevant properties are semantic and emergent.

    \item \textbf{Step-limited simulation is fundamentally insufficient} for $\BB(6)$. The function has entered a regime where outputs require the Knuth arrow hierarchy to express, and discovery requires algebraic analysis, not faster simulators.
\end{enumerate}

The Busy Beaver problem at $n = 6$ stands at the intersection of computability theory, number theory, and formal verification.
Finding new records will require advances not in raw computation, but in our ability to automatically recognize and verify the algebraic structures that give rise to astronomical computations.
The tools, framework, and negative results documented here provide a foundation for such investigations.
