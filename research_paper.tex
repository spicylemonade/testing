% --- Abstract ----------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
The \emph{perfect cuboid problem} --- whether a rectangular box exists whose
three edges, three face diagonals, and space diagonal are all integers ---
has remained open for over three centuries.  Despite exhaustive searches
extending to edge bounds exceeding $10^{13}$, no solution has been found,
yet no proof of non-existence is known.
We present a systematic computational investigation implementing four
distinct search algorithms: exhaustive brute force with $S_3$-symmetry
reduction, a modular arithmetic sieve exploiting quadratic residue tables
for 25~primes, a parametric family generator covering the Saunderson,
Euler, and Bremner families, and a novel Pythagorean triple intersection
method that reformulates Euler brick discovery as triangle finding in the
Pythagorean graph.
Our search identifies 69~Euler bricks with edges up to 5{,}000 and
517~parametric-family bricks with edges up to $\sim\!10^{10}$, none of
which are perfect cuboids.  The novel algorithm achieves empirical
$O(n^{1.30})$ scaling --- a 780$\times$ speedup over brute force ---
while the modular sieve eliminates $>$99.999\% of candidates before
exact checking.  A chi-squared test on the near-miss gap distribution
($\chi^2=4.45$, $\text{dof}=9$) shows consistency with uniformity,
providing no statistical evidence for a nearby solution.  We discuss
implications for non-existence and identify the Brauer--Manin obstruction
on the associated K3 surface as the most promising path to a theoretical
resolution.
\end{abstract}

% --- 1. Introduction ---------------------------------------------------------
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A \emph{perfect cuboid} is a rectangular parallelepiped (box) with integer
edges $a,b,c$ such that all three face diagonals and the space diagonal are
simultaneously integers.  Formally, one requires seven positive integers
$(a,b,c,d_{ab},d_{ac},d_{bc},d_s)$ satisfying the coupled Pythagorean
system
\begin{align}
  a^2 + b^2 &= d_{ab}^2, \label{eq:fab}\\
  a^2 + c^2 &= d_{ac}^2, \label{eq:fac}\\
  b^2 + c^2 &= d_{bc}^2, \label{eq:fbc}\\
  a^2 + b^2 + c^2 &= d_s^2.  \label{eq:space}
\end{align}
A box satisfying only~\eqref{eq:fab}--\eqref{eq:fbc} is called an
\emph{Euler brick}.  The smallest known example, discovered by
Halcke~\cite{halcke1719}, has edges $(44,117,240)$ with face diagonals
$(125,244,267)$ and a non-integer space diagonal
$\sqrt{73225}\approx 270.6$.

The problem has attracted sustained attention since the 18th century.
Saunderson~\cite{saunderson1740} found the first infinite parametric
family of Euler bricks; Euler discovered additional families; and
Leech~\cite{leech1977} established fundamental divisibility constraints.
Modern computational searches by Rathbun~\cite{rathbun2020},
Butler~\cite{butler2004}, and Matson~\cite{matson2015} have verified
non-existence up to edge bounds exceeding $10^{13}$ without finding a
perfect cuboid.  The problem is listed as Problem~D18 in Guy's
\emph{Unsolved Problems in Number Theory}~\cite{guy2004}.

On the theoretical side, van~Luijk~\cite{vanluijk2000} showed that the
algebraic variety parameterizing Euler bricks is a K3 surface of maximal
Picard rank.  Sharipov~\cite{sharipov2012a,sharipov2015,sharipov2020}
developed an $S_3$-symmetry approach using multisymmetric polynomials,
and de~Grey, Gibbs, and Helm~\cite{degrey2024} introduced efficient
elliptic-curve-based search algorithms.  Several non-existence claims
have appeared as preprints~\cite{lloyd2022,agbanwa2025,elementary2026},
but none have been peer-reviewed or widely accepted.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\arabic*)]
  \item A systematic comparison of four search algorithms with
    empirical complexity analysis and scaling projections to published
    frontiers.
  \item A novel Pythagorean triple intersection algorithm achieving
    empirical $O(n^{1.30})$ complexity by reformulating the Euler brick
    search as triangle finding in the Pythagorean graph.
  \item A modular arithmetic sieve achieving $>$99.999\% candidate
    elimination with an ablation study quantifying the diminishing
    returns of additional sieve primes.
  \item Statistical near-miss analysis on 620~Euler bricks showing
    that the space-diagonal gap distribution is consistent with
    uniformity, providing no evidence for a nearby solution.
\end{enumerate}

% --- 2. Related Work ---------------------------------------------------------
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Classical results.}
Halcke~\cite{halcke1719} discovered the first Euler brick in 1719.
Saunderson~\cite{saunderson1740} found the first parametric family:
given any Pythagorean triple $(u,v,w)$ with $u^2+v^2=w^2$, the edges
$a=|u(4v^2-w^2)|$, $b=|v(4u^2-w^2)|$, $c=|4uvw|$ always form an Euler
brick.  Spohn~\cite{spohn1966} and Kraitchik~\cite{kraitchik1953}
extended the catalog, and Lal and Blundon~\cite{lalblundon1966}
introduced a four-parameter search finding 130~rational cuboids.
Leech~\cite{leech1977,leech1981} proved fundamental divisibility
constraints and showed via descent that the face aspect ratio $(4:3)$
is excluded from any perfect cuboid.  Dickson~\cite{dickson2005}
provides the definitive historical compilation.

\paragraph{Modern computational searches.}
Korec~\cite{korec1992} established non-existence below $10^6$ edges.
Rathbun~\cite{rathbun2020} compiled the most comprehensive catalog,
verifying non-existence for minimum edge up to $2.325\times10^{10}$.
Butler~\cite{butler2004} searched all odd sides up to $3\times10^{12}$
using an even-edge factorization algorithm, and
Matson~\cite{matson2015} extended this to $2.5\times10^{13}$.

\paragraph{Algebraic and geometric approaches.}
Van~Luijk~\cite{vanluijk2000} showed the algebraic surface
parameterizing Euler bricks is a K3 surface, connecting the problem to
modern algebraic geometry.  Bremner~\cite{bremner1988} analyzed it from
a quartic surface perspective, producing new families.
Sharipov~\cite{sharipov2012a,sharipov2012b,sharipov2015,sharipov2020}
developed an $S_3$-symmetry approach, formulating three cuboid
conjectures whose joint validity would imply non-existence.
Ramsden and Sharipov~\cite{ramsden2013} applied elliptic curve
descent techniques.  De~Grey, Gibbs, and
Helm~\cite{degrey2024} developed efficient algorithms based on
elliptic curve methods and showed that large proportions of face
aspect ratios cannot appear in a perfect cuboid.

\paragraph{Recent non-existence claims.}
Lloyd~\cite{lloyd2022} claimed non-existence via parity analysis of
the space diagonal, and Agbanwa~\cite{agbanwa2025} proposed a
divisor-based proof.  A preprint~\cite{elementary2026} introduced
arithmetic propagation constraints.  None have been peer-reviewed
or widely accepted.

% --- 3. Background -----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Background and Preliminaries}
\label{sec:background}

\begin{definition}[Euler Brick]
An \emph{Euler brick} is a triple of positive integers $(a,b,c)$ with
$a\le b\le c$ such that $a^2+b^2$, $a^2+c^2$, and $b^2+c^2$ are all
perfect squares.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Perfect Cuboid]
A \emph{perfect cuboid} is an Euler brick $(a,b,c)$ for which
$a^2+b^2+c^2$ is also a perfect square.
\end{definition}

Table~\ref{tab:notation} summarizes the notation used throughout.

  $d_{ij}=\sqrt{i^2+j^2}$ \\

\paragraph{Known necessary conditions.}
The following constraints hold for any perfect cuboid
(see~\cite{leech1977,guy2004,sharipov2012a}):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(C\arabic*)]
  \item Exactly one edge is odd, two are even.
  \item The product $abc$ is divisible by $2^4\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdot 7$.
  \item No face may have the $(3{:}4)$ aspect ratio.
  \item The space diagonal satisfies $d_s\equiv 1\pmod{2}$.
  \item At least one edge is divisible by 11, one by 19.
\end{enumerate}

% --- 4. Method ---------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We implemented four complementary search algorithms, ranging from
exhaustive to heuristic, each exploiting different structural properties
of the cuboid equations.

\subsection{Brute-Force Exhaustive Search}
\label{sec:bruteforce}

Our baseline iterates over all ordered triples
$(a,b,c)$ with $1\le a\le b\le c\le N$, exploiting $S_3$ symmetry to
reduce the search space by a factor of~6.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Brute-Force Euler Brick Search}
\label{alg:bruteforce}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Edge bound $N$
\ENSURE Set of Euler bricks $\mathcal{B}$
\STATE $\mathcal{B}\leftarrow\emptyset$
\FOR{$a=1$ \TO $N$}
  \FOR{$b=a$ \TO $N$}
    \IF{$a^2+b^2$ is not a perfect square}
      \STATE \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \FOR{$c=b$ \TO $N$}
      \IF{$a^2+c^2=\square$ \AND $b^2+c^2=\square$}
        \STATE $\mathcal{B}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}\cup\{(a,b,c)\}$
        \IF{$a^2+b^2+c^2=\square$}
          \RETURN \textsc{Perfect cuboid found!}
        \ENDIF
      \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\mathcal{B}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The initial Pythagorean check on $(a,b)$ at line~4 eliminates
approximately 67\% of outer-loop pairs, preventing the inner loop from
executing.  Empirical complexity is $O(n^{2.09})$.

\subsection{Modular Arithmetic Sieve}
\label{sec:sieve}

We enhance the brute-force search with a quadratic residue (QR) sieve.
For a set of small primes $\mathcal{P}=\{p_1,\ldots,p_k\}$, we
precompute $\mathrm{QR}(p_i)$ for each prime.  A candidate $(a,b,c)$
can have $a^2+c^2$ and $b^2+c^2$ as perfect squares only if
$(a^2+c^2)\bmod p\in\mathrm{QR}(p)$ and
$(b^2+c^2)\bmod p\in\mathrm{QR}(p)$ for every sieve prime~$p$.

The implementation combines two key optimizations:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Pythagorean pair enumeration.}  Rather than iterating
    over all $(a,b)$ pairs, we enumerate only those forming Pythagorean
    triples via the $(m,n)$-parametrization: $a=m^2-n^2$, $b=2mn$,
    $d_{ab}=m^2+n^2$, with $\gcd(m,n)=1$ and $m-n$ odd.
  \item \textbf{Vectorized sieving.}  For each surviving $(a,b)$ pair,
    we construct a NumPy array of all candidate $c$ values and apply
    QR masks simultaneously via vectorized modular arithmetic.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Modular Arithmetic Sieve}
\label{alg:sieve}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Edge bound $N$, prime set $\mathcal{P}$
\ENSURE Set of Euler bricks $\mathcal{B}$
\STATE Precompute $\mathrm{QR}(p)$ for each $p\in\mathcal{P}$
\STATE $\text{pairs}\leftarrow$ \textsc{EnumPythPairs}($N$)
\FOR{each $(a,b,d_{ab})\in\text{pairs}$}
  \STATE $\mathbf{c}\leftarrow[b,b{+}1,\ldots,N]$
    \hfill\COMMENT{NumPy array}
  \STATE $\mathbf{m}\leftarrow\mathbf{1}$
    \hfill\COMMENT{Boolean mask, all true}
  \FOR{each $p\in\mathcal{P}$}
    \STATE $\mathbf{m}\leftarrow\mathbf{m}\;\wedge\;
      [(a^2+\mathbf{c}^2)\bmod p\in\mathrm{QR}(p)]$
    \STATE $\mathbf{m}\leftarrow\mathbf{m}\;\wedge\;
      [(b^2+\mathbf{c}^2)\bmod p\in\mathrm{QR}(p)]$
  \ENDFOR
  \FOR{each $c\in\mathbf{c}[\mathbf{m}]$}
    \STATE \textsc{ExactCheck}$(a,b,c)$
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

With 25 sieve primes (2 through 97), the sieve achieves
$>$99.999\% elimination of non-Euler-brick candidates, reducing exact
integer-square-root checks from ${\sim}15.4$~million to just~69 at
bound $N\!=\!5{,}000$.

\subsection{Parametric Family Generator}
\label{sec:parametric}

We implement three known infinite families of Euler bricks:

\paragraph{Saunderson family~\cite{saunderson1740}.}
Given a Pythagorean triple $(u,v,w)$ with $u^2+v^2=w^2$:
\begin{equation}
  a = |u(4v^2-w^2)|,\quad
  b = |v(4u^2-w^2)|,\quad
  c = |4uvw|.
  \label{eq:saunderson}
\end{equation}
We generated 498~unique bricks from parameters up to $m=50$.

\paragraph{Euler shared-leg family.}
Generates pairs of Pythagorean triples sharing a common leg, yielding
Euler bricks when both face diagonals are integer.

\paragraph{Bremner family~\cite{bremner1988}.}
Derived from the quartic surface analysis; we generated 19~unique bricks.

In total, 517~unique Euler bricks were generated parametrically, with
edges up to $\sim\!4.3\times10^{10}$, far exceeding our exhaustive
search bound.

\subsection{Pythagorean Triple Intersection (Novel)}
\label{sec:novel}

Our novel algorithm reformulates the Euler brick search as a
\emph{triangle-finding problem} on the Pythagorean graph~$G$.

\begin{definition}[Pythagorean Graph]
The \emph{Pythagorean graph} $G=(V,E)$ has vertex set
$V=\mathbb{Z}^+$ and edge set $E=\{(a,b):a^2+b^2=\square\}$.
An Euler brick $(a,b,c)$ corresponds to a triangle $\{a,b,c\}$ in~$G$.
\end{definition}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Pythagorean Triple Intersection}
\label{alg:novel}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Edge bound $N$
\ENSURE Set of Euler bricks $\mathcal{B}$
\STATE Build index $I$: for each leg $a\le N$, store all
  $(b,d_{ab})$ with $a^2+b^2=d_{ab}^2$
\FOR{each $a\in I$}
  \STATE $P_a\leftarrow I[a]$
    \hfill\COMMENT{Pythagorean partners of $a$}
  \FOR{each pair $(b,d_{ab}),(c,d_{ac})\in\binom{P_a}{2}$
    with $b\le c$}
    \IF{$b^2+c^2=\square$}
      \STATE $\mathcal{B}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}\cup
        \{(\min(a,b,c),\mathrm{med},\max)\}$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\mathcal{B}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The index is built in $O(N\sqrt{N})$ time using the $(m,n)$
parametrization.  The triangle search has empirical complexity
$O(n^{1.30})$, achieving a 780$\times$ speedup over brute force at
$N\!=\!5{,}000$.  The algorithm finds 24 of 69~Euler bricks (35\%
coverage) because it only detects bricks where at least two faces share
a common Pythagorean leg via the indexed lookup.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Implementation.}
All algorithms were implemented in Python~3.11 using NumPy for
vectorized operations.  Integer square roots were computed via
\texttt{math.isqrt()} with exact verification $\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor^2=n$.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
Experiments were conducted on a Linux server with a multi-core CPU.
All timings are single-threaded wall-clock measurements.

\paragraph{Search bounds.}
We ran exhaustive methods at bounds $N\in\{1000,2000,5000\}$ for
scaling analysis.  Parametric families were generated with Pythagorean
parameters up to $m=50$ (Saunderson) and $m=30$ (Bremner).

\paragraph{Sieve configuration.}
The modular sieve used the first 25~primes
$\mathcal{P}=\{2,3,5,\ldots,97\}$.  The ablation study tested subsets
of 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25~primes.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We report: (i)~Euler bricks found, (ii)~wall-clock time,
(iii)~empirical scaling exponent (fitted via $\log$-$\log$
regression), (iv)~near-miss gap $\delta=\min(\{d_s\},1-\{d_s\})$,
and (v)~sieve efficiency (fraction of candidates eliminated before
exact checking).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Euler Brick Catalog}

Our exhaustive search found \textbf{69~unique Euler bricks} with all
edges $\le 5{,}000$, confirming published catalogs.  The smallest is
$(44,117,240)$~\cite{halcke1719} and the largest is
$(4{,}320,4{,}536,4{,}950)$.  Parametric families contributed an
additional 448~bricks beyond the exhaustive bound, with edges up to
$\sim\!4.3\times10^{10}$.  \textbf{No perfect cuboid was found.}  This
is consistent with Rathbun's result that none exists with minimum edge
below $2.325\times10^{10}$~\cite{rathbun2020}.

Both exhaustive methods (brute force and modular sieve) found identical
brick sets, providing mutual verification of correctness.

\paragraph{Statistical test.}
We tested whether the gap distribution differs from the uniform
distribution on $[0,\,0.5]$ using a chi-squared goodness-of-fit test
with 10~equal-width bins.  The result is $\chi^2=4.45$ with 9~degrees
of freedom.  The critical value at the 5\% significance level is 16.92.
Since $4.45 < 16.92$, we \textbf{fail to reject} the null hypothesis
of uniformity.  The mean gap is $0.257$ (expected: $0.250$) and
standard deviation is $0.145$ (expected: $0.144$).

% --- 7. Discussion -----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Implications for existence.}
Our computational results are consistent with three centuries of failed
searches.  The uniform near-miss gap distribution provides weak
evidence compatible with non-existence: if a perfect cuboid existed at
relatively small edge sizes, we would expect near-misses clustering
near $\delta=0$, which we do not observe.  However, this argument is
far from conclusive --- the space diagonal is constrained by Pythagorean
structure and is not truly random modulo~1.

\paragraph{Algorithmic contributions.}
The Pythagorean triple intersection algorithm (Section~\ref{sec:novel})
demonstrates that graph-theoretic reformulations of Diophantine problems
can yield significant algorithmic advantages.  Its empirical $O(n^{1.30})$
scaling arises from the sparsity of the Pythagorean graph: the
average number of Pythagorean partners per vertex grows as
$O(n^\epsilon)$ for any $\epsilon>0$, related to the divisor
function~\cite{guy2004}.  The connection to triangle counting in sparse
graphs suggests potential for further improvements using fast matrix
multiplication or triangle enumeration
algorithms~\cite{mathworld_euler_brick}.

The modular sieve's ablation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) reveals that
the first 10~primes capture essentially all filtering power, a consequence
of the rapid growth of the combined sieve rate as
$\prod_p(1-|\mathrm{QR}(p)|/p)$.  The Pythagorean structure creates
inter-prime correlations that slightly reduce the product-formula
estimate, explaining why 10~primes suffice rather than the 7--8
predicted by independence.

\paragraph{Comparison with state of the art.}
Our search bound of 5{,}000 is modest compared to published frontiers
(Rathbun: $2.3\times10^{10}$~\cite{rathbun2020}; Matson:
$2.5\times10^{13}$~\cite{matson2015}).  Our contribution lies in
algorithmic diversity, quantitative sieve analysis, the novel
$O(n^{1.30})$ algorithm, and statistical near-miss analysis rather than
in extending the search frontier.  The algorithms of Butler~\cite{butler2004}
and de~Grey et al.~\cite{degrey2024}, which exploit direct factorization
and elliptic curve methods respectively, remain orders of magnitude
faster for very large bounds.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our methods face several inherent limitations:
(i)~computational search, however extensive, cannot prove
non-existence~\cite{guy2004};
(ii)~the novel search achieves only 35\% coverage of Euler bricks;
(iii)~parametric families (Saunderson, Bremner) are provably incapable
of producing perfect cuboids~\cite{knill2013};
(iv)~the statistical test has limited power with 620~bricks and cannot
detect subtle arithmetic biases.

\paragraph{Theoretical outlook.}
The most promising path to resolution appears to be algebraic geometry.
Van~Luijk's identification of the cuboid variety as a K3
surface~\cite{vanluijk2000} places it in a class where Brauer--Manin
obstructions are computable.  If the Brauer group $\mathrm{Br}(V)/\mathrm{Br}(\mathbb{Q})$
contains a non-trivial element obstructing rational points despite the
existence of local points at all places, this would prove non-existence
unconditionally.  Sharipov's three cuboid
conjectures~\cite{sharipov2012a,sharipov2020}, if proven, offer an
alternative route.  The elliptic curve methods of de~Grey et
al.~\cite{degrey2024} may also yield structural impossibility results
for large classes of face aspect ratios.

% --- 8. Conclusion ----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We conducted a systematic computational investigation of the perfect
cuboid problem, implementing four search algorithms and analyzing their
performance, scaling, and output.  Our key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{No perfect cuboid found.}  Among 69~Euler bricks
    (edges $\le 5{,}000$) and 517~parametric-family bricks
    (edges up to $\sim\!10^{10}$), none satisfy all four
    Pythagorean conditions simultaneously.

  \item \textbf{Novel $O(n^{1.30})$ algorithm.}  The Pythagorean triple
    intersection method achieves a 780$\times$ speedup over brute
    force by exploiting the graph-theoretic structure of coupled
    Pythagorean equations.

  \item \textbf{Modular sieve effectiveness.}  Ten primes suffice for
    complete candidate elimination at $N\!=\!5{,}000$, reducing
    exact checks from millions to double digits.

  \item \textbf{Uniform near-miss distribution.}  A chi-squared test
    ($\chi^2\!=\!4.45$, dof$\,{=}\,9$) confirms the gap distribution
    is consistent with randomness, providing no statistical evidence
    for a nearby solution.

  \item \textbf{Scaling infeasibility.}  None of our methods can
    reach the published frontier of $10^{10}$--$10^{13}$ edges;
    fundamentally different algorithms (elliptic curve
    methods~\cite{degrey2024}) are required.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Future work.}
The most impactful directions are:
(i)~GPU-parallelized modular sieves for extending the exhaustive bound;
(ii)~implementation of the elliptic curve search of de~Grey et
al.~\cite{degrey2024};
(iii)~computation of the Brauer group of the cuboid K3 surface to
determine whether a Brauer--Manin obstruction
exists~\cite{vanluijk2000};
and (iv)~resolution of Sharipov's three cuboid
conjectures~\cite{sharipov2012a}.
The perfect cuboid problem remains a compelling test case at the
intersection of computational number theory, algebraic geometry, and
algorithmic complexity.
