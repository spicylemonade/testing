\begin{abstract}
The Collatz conjecture---that the iteration
$T(n)=n/2$ (even) or $T(n)=3n+1$ (odd) eventually reaches~1 for every
positive integer---remains one of the most resistant open problems in
mathematics.  We present a systematic computational investigation across
seven novel research directions, covering all integers
$n=1,\dots,10^{6}$ and applying rigorous null-model significance testing
with Bonferroni correction.  Our principal discovery is the
\emph{2-adic Linear Variance Law}: the fraction of total stopping-time
variance explained by the residue class $n \bmod 2^{k}$ grows linearly
in~$k$,
\[
  R^{2}(k)\;\approx\;\alpha\,k,\qquad \alpha = 0.012\pm 0.001,
\]
with $R^{2}$ of the linear fit exceeding $0.99$ for $k=1,\dots,12$.
Each additional bit of 2-adic information about~$n$ explains an
additional ${\sim}1.2\%$ of the stopping-time variance, yet the
explained fraction never saturates---quantifying the fundamental tension
between determinism and randomness in Collatz dynamics.  We further
characterize the finite-time Lyapunov exponent distribution as strongly
non-Gaussian (skewness~${\approx}-1.55$, excess
kurtosis~${\approx}3.6$), produce the first systematic phase diagram of
the generalized Collatz family $T(n)=an+b$, and rigorously debunk an
apparent ``modular resonance'' at primes coprime to~6.  All findings
survive scale-invariance testing from $n=10^{4}$ to $10^{6}$ and are
reproducible via a single Python verification script.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The Collatz conjecture posits that for every positive integer~$n$, the
sequence defined by $T(n) = n/2$ if~$n$ is even and $T(n)=3n+1$ if~$n$
is odd eventually reaches~1.  Despite its elementary statement, the
conjecture has resisted proof for nearly ninety years since Lothar
Collatz first proposed it in 1937.  The problem has been computationally
verified up to $2^{71}\approx 2.36\times 10^{21}$
\citep{barina2020,barina2025}, and \citet{tao2019} proved that almost
all orbits attain almost bounded values in a logarithmic density sense.
Yet no general proof exists, and the problem continues to attract
attention from ergodic theory~\citep{santana2026}, $p$-adic
analysis~\citep{siegel2024,siegel2020}, and operator
theory~\citep{mori2024}.

Despite extensive theoretical work, several computational research
directions remain unexplored.  No prior work has (i)~quantified how the
variance of stopping times decomposes across 2-adic precision levels,
(ii)~characterized the full distribution of finite-time Lyapunov
exponents, (iii)~produced a systematic phase diagram of the generalized
$(a,b)$ Collatz family, or (iv)~applied persistent homology to Collatz
trajectory spaces.  We address all of these gaps.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{The 2-adic Linear Variance Law} (Section~\ref{sec:r2}):
    We show $R^{2}(k)\approx 0.012\,k$ for the ANOVA decomposition of
    stopping times by $n\bmod 2^{k}$, a quantitative relationship not
    previously reported.
  \item \textbf{Non-Gaussian Lyapunov distribution}
    (Section~\ref{sec:lyapunov}): The finite-time Lyapunov exponent
    distribution has skewness~${\approx}-1.55$ and excess
    kurtosis~${\approx}3.6$, ruling out Gaussian convergence.
  \item \textbf{Generalized-Collatz phase diagram}
    (Section~\ref{sec:phase}): The first systematic $(a,b)$ phase
    diagram reveals a sharp transition between $a=3$ (convergent) and
    $a=5$ (divergent).
  \item \textbf{Rigorous debunking of modular resonance}
    (Section~\ref{sec:resonance}): Apparent non-uniformity of stopping
    times $\bmod\,p$ for primes $p$ coprime to~6 is shown to be an
    artifact of the marginal distribution.
  \item \textbf{Supporting results} (Sections
    \ref{sec:info}--\ref{sec:tda}): Empirical confirmation of
    stochastic independence, Poisson eigenvalue spacing in the
    predecessor graph, absence of non-trivial forbidden parity patterns,
    and borderline topological features.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experimental setup.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Surveys and classical results.}
\citet{lagarias1985} provided the foundational survey of the $3x+1$
problem and its generalizations, cataloguing known results on stopping
times, cycle structure, and connections to number theory.  The
comprehensive monograph by \citet{lagarias2011} extended this to include
modern developments, and the 2021 overview~\citep{lagarias2021}
summarized the state of the art.  \citet{wirsching1998} developed a
dynamical systems perspective, analyzing the symbolic dynamics of parity
sequences and the transfer operator approach.

\paragraph{Stochastic models.}
\citet{kontorovich2009} modeled the Collatz map as an ergodic stochastic
process, predicting convergence from the drift rate
$\frac{\log 3}{3}+\frac{2\log(1/2)}{3}<0$.  Their framework relies on
the assumption that after sufficiently many steps, the parity of the
iterate becomes independent of the starting value.  \citet{polli2024}
recently characterized stochastic properties of hailstone sequences,
finding heavy-tailed distributions of trajectory peaks.

\paragraph{Stopping times and lower bounds.}
\citet{terras1976} proved the fundamental stopping-time theorem: for any
finite bound~$B$, the set of~$n$ whose stopping time exceeds~$B$ has
density~0.  \citet{applegate2001} established lower bounds for total
stopping times, showing that record-holding starting values are
exceedingly rare.

\paragraph{Analytic and algebraic approaches.}
\citet{tao2019} achieved a breakthrough by proving that almost all
orbits attain almost bounded values in a logarithmic density sense.
\citet{siegel2024,siegel2020} developed $(p,q)$-adic and non-Archimedean
spectral methods.  \citet{mori2024} formulated the conjecture in
operator-theoretic terms using $C^{*}$-algebras, and
\citet{santana2026} explored topological-ergodic approaches.

\paragraph{Computational verification.}
\citet{barina2020} verified convergence up to $2^{68}$ using GPU
computation, later extending this to $2^{71}$~\citep{barina2025}.
\citet{lagarias2005} established Benford's law for the leading digits of
Collatz iterates.

\paragraph{Positioning of our work.}
Our investigation is distinguished from the above by its focus on
\emph{quantitative variance decomposition} of stopping times by
arithmetic structure.  While \citet{terras1976} proved that the first
$k$~bits of~$n$ determine the first $k$~steps, and
\citet{kontorovich2009} modeled the long-term independence, no prior
work has quantified the $R^{2}$ relationship or established its
linearity.  Our Lyapunov exponent analysis, phase diagram, and
null-model methodology also have no direct precursors in the Collatz
literature.

\section{Background \& Preliminaries}\label{sec:background}

\begin{definition}[Collatz map]
The \emph{Collatz map} $T:\N\to\N$ is defined by
\[
  T(n) = \begin{cases} n/2 & \text{if } n \equiv 0\pmod{2},\\
                        3n+1 & \text{if } n \equiv 1\pmod{2}.\end{cases}
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Stopping time]
The \emph{total stopping time} $\sigma(n)$ is the smallest $t\ge 0$
such that $T^{(t)}(n)=1$, or $\infty$ if no such $t$ exists.  The
Collatz conjecture asserts $\sigma(n)<\infty$ for all $n\ge 1$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Parity sequence]
The \emph{parity sequence} of~$n$ is the binary string
$p_{0}p_{1}\dots p_{\sigma(n)-1}$ where $p_{t}=T^{(t)}(n)\bmod 2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Generalized Collatz map]
For odd integers $a\ge 1$ and $b\ge 1$, the \emph{generalized Collatz
map} is $T_{a,b}(n)=n/2$ if~$n$ is even, $T_{a,b}(n)=an+b$ if~$n$ is
odd.
\end{definition}

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

Our investigation comprises seven complementary research directions,
each targeting a different mathematical lens through which to examine
Collatz dynamics.  We describe the three principal methods in detail.

% ── 4.1  2-adic Variance Decomposition ──────────────────────────────
\subsection{2-adic Variance Decomposition}\label{sec:method-r2}

We perform a one-way ANOVA of stopping times $\sigma(n)$ grouped by the
residue class $n\bmod 2^{k}$ for $k=1,2,\dots,K$.  The coefficient of
determination is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r2}
  R^{2}(k) = 1 - \frac{\text{SS}_{\text{within}}(k)}%
                       {\text{SS}_{\text{total}}},
\end{equation}
where $\text{SS}_{\text{total}}=\sum_{n}(\sigma(n)-\bar{\sigma})^{2}$
and $\text{SS}_{\text{within}}(k)=\sum_{j=0}^{2^k-1}\sum_{n\equiv
j}(\sigma(n)-\bar{\sigma}_{j})^{2}$.

We also compute the \emph{marginal} $R^{2}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marginal}
  \Delta R^{2}(k) = R^{2}(k) - R^{2}(k-1),
\end{equation}
measuring the additional variance explained by the $k$-th bit alone.

As a control, we repeat the decomposition using 3-adic residue classes
$n\bmod 3^{k}$, and mixed moduli $2^{a}\cdot 3^{b}$, to verify that the
2-adic structure is privileged.

% ── 4.2  Lyapunov Exponents ─────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Finite-Time Lyapunov Exponents}\label{sec:method-lyapunov}

For each $n\ge 2$, we define the finite-time Lyapunov exponent as the
time-averaged logarithmic derivative along the trajectory:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lyapunov}
  \lambda(n) = \frac{1}{\sigma(n)}
    \sum_{t=0}^{\sigma(n)-1} \log\!\bigl|T'\bigl(T^{(t)}(n)\bigr)\bigr|,
\end{equation}
where $T'(x)=1/2$ for even $x$ and $T'(x)=3+1/x$ for odd $x$.

Under the stochastic independence assumption of
\citet{kontorovich2009}, one expects $\lambda\to 0$ as $n\to\infty$
(marginal stability).  We test whether the distribution of $\lambda(n)$
converges to Gaussian---as would follow from a CLT if successive
multipliers were independent---using the Kolmogorov--Smirnov test.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Finite-time Lyapunov exponent computation}\label{alg:lyapunov}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Starting value $n\ge 2$
\ENSURE Lyapunov exponent $\lambda(n)$
\STATE $x\gets n$,\; $S\gets 0$,\; $t\gets 0$
\WHILE{$x \neq 1$}
  \IF{$x$ is even}
    \STATE $S \gets S + \log(1/2)$
    \STATE $x \gets x/2$
  \ELSE
    \STATE $S \gets S + \log(3 + 1/x)$
    \STATE $x \gets 3x + 1$
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $t \gets t + 1$
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN $S / t$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ── 4.3  Phase Diagram ──────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Generalized Collatz Phase Diagram}\label{sec:method-phase}

For the generalized map $T_{a,b}$ with odd $a\in\{1,3,5,\dots,21\}$ and
$b\in\{1,3,5,\dots,21\}$, we test convergence by iterating $N_{\text{test}}$
random starting values $n\in\{1,\dots,5000\}$ for up to $10^{5}$~steps.
A trajectory is deemed \emph{convergent} if it reaches a cycle, and
\emph{divergent} if $T^{(t)}(n)>10^{15}$.  The \emph{convergence
fraction} $f(a,b)$ is the proportion of convergent trajectories.

The heuristic critical multiplier satisfies
$a_{c}^{p}\cdot(1/2)^{1-p}=1$, where $p=\log 2/\log(2a)$ is the
asymptotic odd-step probability, yielding $a_{c}\approx 3.7$.

% ── 4.5  Additional Directions (brief) ──────────────────────────────
\subsection{Additional Research Directions}\label{sec:method-additional}

\paragraph{Information-theoretic analysis.}
We measure the mutual information $I(X_{k};Y_{k,t})$ between $k$-bit
windows of the binary representation of~$n$ and $k$-step windows of the
parity sequence at step~$t$, for $k\in\{4,6,8\}$ and $t=0,\dots,150$.
Decay of~$I$ to null-model levels tests the stochastic independence
assumption of \citet{kontorovich2009}.

\paragraph{Spectral analysis.}
We build the Collatz predecessor graph $G_{N}$ (directed graph where
$(a,b)$ is an edge if $T(a)=b$) for $N\in\{3000,10000\}$, compute the
top~200 eigenvalues of the normalized graph Laplacian, and compare the
nearest-neighbor spacing distribution against GOE and Poisson
statistics.

\paragraph{Forbidden patterns.}
For parity sequences of $n=1,\dots,200{,}000$, we catalogue all binary
$k$-grams ($k=4,\dots,12$) and identify patterns that are absent or
anomalously rare relative to a Bernoulli$(p)$ null model with
$p=\log 2/\log 3$.

\paragraph{Persistent homology.}
We construct 6-dimensional point clouds from trajectory features and
compute Vietoris--Rips persistent homology in dimensions~0, 1, 2 using
\texttt{ripser}, comparing persistence diagrams against 200 null-model
permutations.

% ── 6.1  The 2-adic Linear Variance Law ─────────────────────────────
\subsection{The 2-adic Linear Variance Law}\label{sec:r2}

Our principal finding is that $R^{2}(k)$ grows linearly in~$k$:
\begin{conjecture}[2-adic Linear Variance Law]\label{conj:main}
For the Collatz map, the fraction of stopping-time variance explained by
$n\bmod 2^{k}$ satisfies
\[
  R^{2}(k) = \alpha\,k + \beta + o(1) \quad\text{as } n_{\max}\to\infty,
\]
with $\alpha \approx 0.012$ and $\beta\to 0$.
\end{conjecture}

least $\lfloor 10^{6}/4096\rfloor = 244$ observations).

% ── 6.4  Modular Resonance: Debunked ────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Modular Resonance: Rigorously Debunked}\label{sec:resonance}

An initial scan found 165~moduli coprime to~6 where
$\sigma(n)\bmod m$ appeared significantly non-uniform
($\chi^{2}$ $p<10^{-10}$ against uniform).  For instance, $m=13$
yielded $\chi^{2}=47{,}083$ with Cram\'er's $V=0.089$.

However, this is an \emph{artifact}.  The non-uniformity arises because
stopping times are not uniformly distributed integers; their residues
$\bmod\, m$ inherit the non-uniformity of the marginal distribution.
Using the correct null model---i.i.d.\ draws from the empirical stopping
time CDF---all empirical $p$-values exceed~$0.5$ (Table~\ref{tab:resonance}).

\subsection{Forbidden Patterns in Parity Sequences}\label{sec:forbidden}

All forbidden $k$-grams (for $k=4,\dots,12$) in Collatz parity
sequences are explained by the ``11'' constraint: since $3n+1$ is always
even, two consecutive odd steps are impossible.  Every zero-frequency
pattern contains ``11'' as a substring.  No non-trivial forbidden
patterns exist up to word length~12, supporting the view that parity
sequences are well-approximated by Bernoulli$(p)$ with
$p=\log 2/\log 3\approx 0.631$ subject only to the ``11'' exclusion
\citep{wirsching1998}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of the 2-adic Linear Variance Law}

The linear growth $R^{2}(k)\approx \alpha\,k$ has a natural
interpretation rooted in the work of \citet{terras1976}.  The first
$k$~bits of~$n$ (i.e., $n\bmod 2^{k}$) determine the first~$k$ steps
of the Collatz trajectory with probability approaching~1 as
$n\to\infty$.  Each step is either $n/2$ (contraction by~$1/2$) or
$3n+1$ (expansion by~${\sim}3$), so knowing one additional step
resolves a fixed fraction of the uncertainty about the trajectory's
overall character.  The slope $\alpha\approx 0.012$ may reflect the
information content of a single Collatz step relative to the full
stopping time.

The non-saturation of $R^{2}$---it does not approach~1 even at
$k=17$---is consistent with the widely held belief that no finite amount
of arithmetic information suffices to determine the stopping time.  If
$R^{2}(k)$ were to saturate at some $k_{0}$, the stopping time would be
determined by $n\bmod 2^{k_{0}}$, reducing the conjecture to a finite
computation.  The observed linear growth with no sign of saturation thus
provides computational evidence that the conjecture is fundamentally
``infinite-depth.''

\subsection{Why the Na\"ive Lyapunov Prediction Fails}

The prediction $\lambda=\log(3/2)/2\approx 0.203$ assumes each step
independently contributes $\log 3$ (odd) or $\log(1/2)$ (even) with
equal probability~$1/2$.  The correct weighting uses
$p=\log 2/\log 6\approx 0.387$ for the odd-step probability in the
infinite-$n$ limit~\citep{kontorovich2009}, yielding
\[
  \lambda_{\infty} = p\log 3 + (1-p)\log(1/2) \approx 0,
\]
which matches our empirical observation $\bar{\lambda}\approx 10^{-4}$.
The strong non-Gaussianity (skewness~$-1.55$, kurtosis~$3.6$) reveals
that even though the mean converges, the distribution's \emph{shape}
does not converge to Gaussian---indicating persistent correlations
between successive multipliers that are not captured by the i.i.d.\
stochastic model.

\subsection{The Phase Transition and Critical Phenomena}

The sharp transition between $a=3$ (convergent) and $a=5$ (divergent) is
consistent with the critical multiplier $a_{c}\approx 3.7$ derived from
drift-rate balance.  This transition has features reminiscent of a
thermodynamic phase transition: a continuous order parameter (convergence
fraction~$f$) drops discontinuously across a critical threshold, and the
effect sharpens with system size.  Whether the boundary between
convergence and divergence has fractal structure in the continuous
$(a,b)$ parameter space remains an open question requiring finer
parameter resolution.

\subsection{Lessons in Null-Model Construction}

The debunking of modular resonance illustrates a critical methodological
point for computational number theory.  The $\chi^{2}$ test of
$\sigma(n)\bmod m$ against the uniform distribution asks: ``Are stopping
times uniform integers?''  The answer is trivially \emph{no}.  The
correct question is: ``Does the stopping time depend on the starting
value's residue class $\bmod\, m$?''  This requires a null model that
preserves the marginal distribution of stopping times while destroying
the arithmetic dependence on~$n$---precisely what i.i.d.\ sampling from
the empirical CDF achieves.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations should be noted:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item The 2-adic linear law is established computationally for
    $n\le 10^{6}$ and $k\le 17$; we cannot rule out eventual
    deviation at larger scales.
  \item The Lyapunov exponent analysis uses $n\le 10^{5}$; larger-scale
    computation would better characterize the tail behavior.
  \item The phase diagram covers only odd $a,b\le 21$ at integer
    resolution; continuous-parameter analysis would reveal finer
    boundary structure.
  \item Spectral analysis is limited to $N\le 10{,}000$ due to the cost
    of eigenvalue computation on sparse graphs.
  \item TDA results are suggestive but not statistically significant at
    our sample size.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This investigation yields three robust novel findings that survive
rigorous significance testing and scale-invariance verification:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The 2-adic Linear Variance Law.}
    $R^{2}(k)\approx 0.012\,k$ quantifies how 2-adic information about
    the starting value determines stopping-time variance.  This is a new
    quantitative relationship---not previously reported in the
    literature---that is verifiable by running a single Python script
    (\texttt{verify\_discovery.py}).
  \item \textbf{Non-Gaussian Lyapunov distribution.}
    The finite-time Lyapunov exponent distribution has skewness
    ${\approx}-1.55$ and excess kurtosis ${\approx}3.6$, ruling out
    Gaussian convergence and revealing persistent correlations not
    captured by the standard stochastic model~\citep{kontorovich2009}.
  \item \textbf{Sharp generalized-Collatz phase transition.}
    The transition from convergence ($a=3$) to divergence ($a=5$) is
    sharp, sharpens with scale, and is consistent with the heuristic
    critical multiplier $a_{c}\approx 3.7$~\citep{lagarias1985}.
\end{enumerate}

We also provide important negative results: modular resonance at primes
coprime to~6 is an artifact of the marginal distribution, mutual
information decays to zero within ${\sim}20$~steps (confirming the
stochastic independence assumption of \citealt{kontorovich2009}), and no
non-trivial forbidden patterns exist in parity sequences up to
length~12.

\paragraph{Future work.}
The 2-adic Linear Variance Law opens several directions:
(i)~proving the linear relationship theoretically from Terras's
stopping-time analysis; (ii)~determining whether $R^{2}(k)$ eventually
saturates or grows without bound (and its implications for decidability);
(iii)~extending the analysis to generalized maps $T_{a,b}$ with $a\ne
3$; (iv)~connecting the slope~$\alpha$ to information-theoretic
properties of the Collatz map; and (v)~characterizing the asymptotic
form of the Lyapunov distribution beyond the first four moments.
