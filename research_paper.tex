\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The \emph{perfect cuboid problem}---whether a rectangular box exists whose
three edges, three face diagonals, and space diagonal are all positive
integers---has remained open for over three centuries.  We present a
comprehensive computational investigation combining five complementary
search strategies: classical parametric family generation
\citep{saunderson1740,euler1770}, Pythagorean triple decomposition via
common-leg indexing, constraint satisfaction programming with multi-modulus
residue pre-filtering, quadratic residue sieving across 50 primes
\citep{roberts2009}, and a unified pipeline integrating all methods.  Our
combined search discovers 1{,}714 Euler bricks with edges up to $10^5$ in
4.5~seconds, representing a speedup exceeding $10^{6}\times$ over na\"ive
brute-force enumeration.  We confirm the null result: \emph{no perfect
cuboid exists within our search bounds}.  Statistical analysis of near-miss
distributions reveals a weak negative power-law trend (slope $= -0.30$,
$R^2 = 0.025$, $p = 0.024$) in near-miss scores versus edge magnitude,
offering tentative evidence that larger Euler bricks come marginally closer
to satisfying the space-diagonal condition.  We critically review three
recent non-existence proof attempts
\citep{lloyd2022,wyss2015,yelle2026}---finding none conclusive---and employ
cross-domain concept evolution to identify novel algorithmic strategies
including spectral-gap-motivated landscape analysis and constraint-graph
sieve design achieving a 99.8\% candidate rejection rate.  We conclude
that the weight of evidence---computational, algebraic-geometric
\citep{stolltesta2010}, and statistical---favors non-existence, though the
problem remains rigorously open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Problem Statement}

A \emph{perfect cuboid} (also called a \emph{perfect box} or
\emph{perfect Euler brick}) is a rectangular parallelepiped with edge
lengths $a, b, c \in \Zp$ such that every face diagonal and the space
diagonal are also positive integers.  Formally, seven unknowns
$(a,b,c,d,e,f,g)$ must satisfy the system
\begin{align}
  a^2 + b^2 &= d^2, \label{eq:face1}\\
  b^2 + c^2 &= e^2, \label{eq:face2}\\
  a^2 + c^2 &= f^2, \label{eq:face3}\\
  a^2 + b^2 + c^2 &= g^2, \label{eq:space}
\end{align}
with $a,b,c,d,e,f,g \in \Zp$ \citep{euler1770,guy2004}.  The problem is
equivalently stated as finding four simultaneous Pythagorean triples
sharing three leg values, or finding a rational point on a specific
algebraic surface of general type \citep{stolltesta2010}.

\subsection{Historical Context}

The problem traces to \citet{halcke1719}, who published the first
known \emph{Euler brick}---a box with integer edges and face diagonals
but irrational space diagonal: $(a,b,c)=(44,117,240)$ with face
diagonals $(125,267,244)$ and space diagonal
$\sqrt{73225}\approx 270.6$.  \citet{saunderson1740} discovered the
first parametric family of Euler bricks, and \citet{euler1770}
extended the theory.  \citet{pocklington1912} proved that Saunderson's
family can never yield a perfect cuboid, a result formalized by
\citet{spohn1972}.

Computational searches have pushed the verified non-existence boundary
to remarkable scales.  \citet{rathbun2017} cataloged over 167{,}000
integer cuboids; \citet{butler_web} searched odd sides to
$3\times 10^{12}$; and \citet{matson2014} established that no perfect
cuboid exists with an odd edge below $2.5\times 10^{13}$.  On the
algebraic-geometric front, \citet{stolltesta2010} proved the cuboid
surface is of general type with geometric Picard rank~64, implying
(under the Bombieri--Lang conjecture) that rational points are not
Zariski-dense---the strongest structural constraint to date.  Their
subsequent work computed the L-function and \'etale cohomology of the
surface \citep{stolltesta2025}.

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\arabic*)]
  \item A unified computational framework implementing five
    complementary search strategies---parametric families, triple
    decomposition, constraint satisfaction, quadratic residue sieving,
    and combined search---within a single benchmarkable pipeline
    (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item Empirical benchmarks demonstrating a $>10^{6}\times$ speedup
    of the combined approach over brute-force enumeration
    (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item A statistical characterization of near-miss distributions,
    including power-law regression and prime-factorization analysis of
    space-diagonal residuals (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Critical assessment of three recent non-existence proof
    attempts (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
  \item Identification of six concrete future research directions
    spanning GPU-accelerated search, Brauer--Manin obstruction
    computation, and formal verification of proof attempts
    (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Parametric Families and Euler Bricks}

Infinite families of Euler bricks have been known since
\citet{saunderson1740}: for any primitive Pythagorean triple $(u,v,w)$,
the triple $a = u|4v^2 - w^2|$, $b = v|4u^2 - w^2|$, $c = 4uvw$ yields
an Euler brick.  \citet{euler1770} composed pairs of Pythagorean triples
to generate further families.  \citet{spohn1972} proved that the ``derived
cuboid'' from Saunderson's family cannot be perfect.  Modern catalogs
contain hundreds of thousands of Euler bricks
\citep{rathbun2017,butler_web}, yet none has an integer space diagonal.

\subsection{Modular Arithmetic Constraints}

\citet{kraitchik1945,kraitchik1947} established foundational constraints:
in a primitive perfect cuboid, exactly one edge is odd, one even edge is
divisible by~4, and another by~16.  \citet{roberts2009} extended these
using quadratic residue analysis, showing that at least one edge must be
divisible by each of 7, 11, and 19, with the product of all edges and
face diagonals divisible by
$2^8 \cdot 3^4 \cdot 5^3 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 \cdot 17 \cdot 19
\cdot 29 \cdot 37$.  Combined, these modular constraints eliminate over
99\% of candidate triples before any arithmetic operations.

\subsection{Algebraic Geometry}

\citet{vanluijk2000} reformulated the problem as finding rational points
on an algebraic surface~$S$, establishing a rank-64 subgroup of the
Picard group.  \citet{stolltesta2010} proved that the minimal
desingularization of~$S$ is a surface of general type with geometric
Picard rank exactly~64.  Under the Bombieri--Lang conjecture, this
implies that rational points are confined to finitely many curves---a
powerful structural constraint.  \citet{stolltesta2025} computed the
L-function and connected the surface's \'etale cohomology to elliptic
modular cusp forms.

\subsection{Computational Searches}

\citet{matson2014} established the state-of-the-art computational bound:
no perfect cuboid with odd edge below $2.5\times 10^{13}$ or smallest
even side below $5\times 10^{11}$.  \citet{rathbun2017} enumerated
167{,}043 cuboids of all types.  \citet{butler_web} covered odd sides to
$3\times 10^{12}$.  All searches yielded a null result.

\subsection{Non-Existence Proof Attempts}

Several non-existence proofs have been claimed but none accepted:
\citet{lloyd2022} argued the space diagonal cannot be odd (contradicting
\citealt{roberts2009}); \citet{wyss2015} used Gaussian integers
(critiqued by \citealt{sharipov2017}); \citet{yelle2026} proposed an
elementary obstruction via ``triangular remainder.''
\citet{sharipov2021} studied the problem through the symmetry group of
the Diophantine system.  The problem remains open \citep{guy2004}.

\section{Background \& Preliminaries}\label{sec:background}
% ============================================================================

\begin{definition}[Perfect Cuboid]
A \emph{perfect cuboid} is a 7-tuple $(a,b,c,d,e,f,g)\in\Zp^7$
satisfying Equations~\eqref{eq:face1}--\eqref{eq:space}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Euler Brick]
An \emph{Euler brick} is a triple $(a,b,c)\in\Zp^3$ satisfying only the
face-diagonal conditions \eqref{eq:face1}--\eqref{eq:face3}, i.e., all
face diagonals are integers but the space diagonal need not be.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Near-Miss Score]
For an Euler brick $(a,b,c)$ with $s = a^2+b^2+c^2$, the
\emph{near-miss score} is
\begin{equation}
  \sigma(a,b,c) = \frac{|s - \lfloor\sqrt{s}\rceil^2|}{s},
  \label{eq:nearmiss}
\end{equation}
where $\lfloor\cdot\rceil$ denotes rounding to the nearest integer.  A
perfect cuboid has $\sigma = 0$.
\end{definition}

Table~\ref{tab:notation} summarizes the notation used throughout.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Notation summary.}\label{tab:notation}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & Meaning \\
\midrule
$a, b, c$     & Edge lengths of the box \\
$d, e, f$     & Face diagonals: $d = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, etc.\ \\
$g$           & Space diagonal: $g = \sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}$ \\
$(m, n)$      & Pythagorean triple parameters, $m > n > 0$, $\gcd(m,n)=1$, $m-n$ odd \\
$\sigma$      & Near-miss score (Eq.~\ref{eq:nearmiss}) \\
$S$           & Cuboid algebraic surface \citep{stolltesta2010} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{proposition}[Pythagorean Parametrization]
Every primitive Pythagorean triple $(x,y,z)$ with $x$ odd, $y$ even,
$z$ the hypotenuse, can be written as
\begin{equation}
  x = m^2 - n^2,\quad y = 2mn,\quad z = m^2 + n^2,
  \label{eq:pyth-param}
\end{equation}
for coprime integers $m > n > 0$ with $m - n$ odd.
\end{proposition}

A perfect cuboid requires three face-diagonal triples $T_1=(a,b,d)$,
$T_2=(b,c,e)$, $T_3=(a,c,f)$ plus the space-diagonal triple
$T_4=(d,c,g)$ (or equivalent permutation), forming a highly
over-determined system.  The fourth equation~\eqref{eq:space} is not
independent---it can be rewritten as $d^2 + c^2 = g^2$---but
simultaneously satisfying all four integer constraints is the core
difficulty.

% ---------- 4.1 Parametric Families -----------------------------------------
\subsection{Parametric Family Generation}\label{sec:parametric}

We implement two classical families in \texttt{src/euler\_brick.py}:

\paragraph{Saunderson family \citep{saunderson1740}.}
For each primitive Pythagorean triple $(u,v,w)$ generated from parameters
$(m,n)$, we compute
\begin{equation}
  a = u|4v^2 - w^2|,\quad b = v|4u^2 - w^2|,\quad c = 4uvw.
  \label{eq:saunderson}
\end{equation}
With $\max(m)=30$, this produces 210 distinct Euler bricks.
\citet{pocklington1912} proved that for this family,
$a^2+b^2+c^2 = w^2(u^4 + 18u^2v^2 + v^4)$, and $u^4+18u^2v^2+v^4$ is
never a perfect square, so no perfect cuboid arises.

\paragraph{Euler two-triple family \citep{euler1770}.}
Compositions of two Pythagorean triples sharing a structural relationship,
via the \emph{Brahmagupta--Fibonacci identity}
$(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2)=(ac+bd)^2+(ad-bc)^2$, generate Euler bricks outside
Saunderson's family (e.g., $(240,252,275)$).  An extended multi-hop
search with $\max(m)=200$ discovers 1{,}238 additional Euler bricks not
found by classical families.

% ---------- 4.2 Modular Filter ----------------------------------------------
\subsection{Multi-Stage Modular Sieve}\label{sec:modsieve}

The modular filter (\texttt{src/modular\_filter.py}) implements the
constraints of \citet{kraitchik1945,kraitchik1947} and
\citet{roberts2009} in four cascaded stages:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{S\arabic*}:, leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Parity filter.}  Reject unless exactly one edge is odd,
    one divisible by~4, one by~16.
  \item \textbf{Mod-24 filter.}  Verify congruence conditions on edge
    residues modulo~24.
  \item \textbf{Mod-48 filter.}  Tighter constraints modulo~48 per
    \citet{roberts2009}.
  \item \textbf{Multi-modulus QR filter.}  For moduli in
    $\{5,7,8,9,16\}$, verify that $a^2+b^2$, $b^2+c^2$, $a^2+c^2$ are
    quadratic residues.
\end{enumerate}

The combined filter achieves 99.8\% rejection on random triples up to
$10^6$ (Table~\ref{tab:sieve}).

% ---------- 4.3 Triple Decomposition ----------------------------------------
\subsection{Pythagorean Triple Decomposition}\label{sec:triple-decomp}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:triple} presents the triple decomposition search.
The key insight is that by indexing all primitive Pythagorean triples by
their leg values, we immediately restrict attention to triples
$(a,b,c)$ where at least two face diagonals are guaranteed integers.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Triple Decomposition Search}\label{alg:triple}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Maximum hypotenuse bound $H$
\ENSURE Set of Euler bricks $\mathcal{E}$, set of perfect cuboids $\mathcal{P}$
\STATE Generate all primitive Pythagorean triples $(x,y,z)$ with $z \le H$
\STATE Build index $\mathcal{I}$: for each leg value $v$, store all triples containing $v$ as a leg
\FOR{each value $v$ in $\mathcal{I}$ with $|\mathcal{I}[v]| \ge 2$}
  \FOR{each pair of triples $T_1, T_2$ sharing leg $v$}
    \STATE Let $a, b$ be the other legs of $T_1, T_2$
    \IF{$\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \in \Z$}
      \STATE Add $(v, a, b)$ to $\mathcal{E}$ \COMMENT{Euler brick}
      \IF{$\sqrt{v^2 + a^2 + b^2} \in \Z$}
        \STATE Add $(v, a, b)$ to $\mathcal{P}$ \COMMENT{Perfect cuboid!}
      \ENDIF
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\mathcal{E}$, $\mathcal{P}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The complexity is $\bigO(T^2/V)$ where $T$ is the number of triples
and $V$ the number of distinct leg values, dramatically outperforming the
$\bigO(N^3)$ brute-force search.

% ---------- 4.4 Constraint Satisfaction -------------------------------------
\subsection{Constraint Satisfaction Approach}\label{sec:csp}

The CSP solver (\texttt{src/constraint\_solver.py}) precomputes valid
residue classes for each variable modulo
$\{16, 9, 5, 7, 11, 13\}$.  Only triples $(a,b,c)$ whose residues
modulo every modulus appear in the valid set are passed to the
arithmetic checks.  This achieves 97\% pre-filtering before any
integer square-root computations.

% ---------- 4.5 Quadratic Residue Sieve ------------------------------------
\subsection{Quadratic Residue Sieve}\label{sec:qrsieve}

The sieve (\texttt{src/quadratic\_sieve.py}) precomputes quadratic
residue sets for 50 primes $p \le 1000$.  For each candidate
$(a,b,c)$, it checks whether $a^2+b^2$, $b^2+c^2$, $a^2+c^2$, and
$a^2+b^2+c^2$ are quadratic residues modulo each prime simultaneously.
The mod-48 stage alone achieves $>99.5\%$ rejection; the full 50-prime
sieve achieves near-100\% rejection on random triples.

% ---------- 4.6 Combined Pipeline -------------------------------------------
\subsection{Combined Search Pipeline}\label{sec:combined}

The combined search (\texttt{src/combined\_search.py}) integrates triple
decomposition with both classical and extended parametric families.
Near-misses are tracked via the \texttt{NearMissTracker} class (top-$K$
sorted by $\sigma$), and all results are logged to a JSONL file for
post-hoc analysis.

% ---------- 4.7 Cross-Domain Concept Evolution ------------------------------
\subsection{Cross-Domain Concept Evolution}\label{sec:conceptevolve}

We applied the \textsc{ConceptEvolve} framework to discover
cross-domain analogies for the perfect cuboid problem.  The framework
produced 10 concept cards, a 10-node semantic bridge graph, and 5 domain
reframings.  Two actionable insights emerged:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Modeling the constraint system as a \emph{treewidth-3 hypergraph

    (Section~\ref{sec:modsieve}).
  \item The \emph{spectral gap} framing motivated the power-law
    regression analysis of near-miss distributions
    (Section~\ref{sec:nearmiss-analysis}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Performance Benchmarks}

The baseline brute-force search achieves $1.27\times10^8$ candidates per
second with the modular filter enabled, but its $\bigO(N^3)$ scaling
makes it impractical beyond $N\approx10^4$.  Triple decomposition
provides $>100\times$ speedup for Euler brick discovery.  The combined
approach covers $[1,10^5]$ in 4.5~s; an equivalent brute-force search
would require approximately $10^{15}/1.27\times10^8 \approx 7.9\times
10^6$~s ($\approx$91~days), yielding a speedup exceeding
$\mathbf{10^{6}\times}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark}).

\subsection{Near-Miss Analysis}\label{sec:nearmiss-analysis}

Statistical analysis of 200 near-miss Euler bricks reveals the
following:

\paragraph{Best near-miss.}
$(a,b,c) = (43440, 45612, 49775)$ achieves $\sigma = 2.98\times10^{-8}$
($a^2+b^2+c^2 = 6{,}445{,}038{,}769$,
$\sqrt{s} \approx 80281.0$).

\paragraph{Power-law regression.}
Fitting $\log\sigma = \beta\log M + \alpha$ where
$M = \max(a,b,c)$ yields slope $\beta = -0.30$, $R^2 = 0.025$,

bricks come marginally closer to being perfect cuboids, consistent with
observations by \citet{matson2014}.  However, the extremely low $R^2$
indicates this trend explains very little variance
(Figure~\ref{fig:nearmiss}).

\paragraph{Prime factorization of residuals.}

space-diagonal residuals (49\% and 52\%, respectively), while larger
primes appear at frequencies consistent with random divisibility.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Evidence Regarding Existence}

The evidence from our investigation, combined with prior work,
overwhelmingly suggests that the perfect cuboid does not exist:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Computational evidence.}  Exhaustive searches up to
    $10^{13}$ \citep{matson2014} and our own search to $10^5$ have found
    zero perfect cuboids among hundreds of thousands of Euler bricks.

  \item \textbf{Algebraic-geometric evidence.}  The Stoll--Testa proof
    that the cuboid surface is of general type \citep{stolltesta2010}
    implies, under the Bombieri--Lang conjecture, that rational points
    are confined to finitely many curves---the strongest structural
    argument for non-existence.

  \item \textbf{Statistical evidence.}  While our near-miss analysis
    shows a slight improvement in near-miss scores at larger magnitudes
    (negative regression slope), the effect is extremely weak
    ($R^2=0.025$).  The prime factorization of residuals shows no
    systematic pattern suggesting convergence toward zero.

  \item \textbf{Proof attempts.}  Three independent proof attempts
    \citep{lloyd2022,wyss2015,yelle2026} target non-existence,
    consistent with the mathematical community's intuition, though none
    has been validated.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Assessment of Non-Existence Proofs}

Table~\ref{tab:proofs} summarizes our critical review of three claimed
non-existence proofs.

\begin{table}[t]

\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Author & Year & Strategy & Status \\
\midrule
\citet{wyss2015}   & 2015 & Gaussian integers  & Refuted \citep{sharipov2017} \\
\citet{lloyd2022}  & 2022 & Odd space diagonal & Unverified (withdrawn, resubmitted) \\
\citet{yelle2026}  & 2026 & Triangular remainder & Too recent; awaiting review \\

\citet{lloyd2022} claimed the space diagonal cannot be odd, contradicting
the result of \citet{roberts2009} that it must be odd.  The argument
relies on coprimality conditions between $d\pm c$ and $g$ that have not
been validated.  \citet{wyss2015} used Gaussian integer factorization,
but \citet{sharipov2017} identified gaps in the handling of units and
associates in $\Z[i]$.  \citet{yelle2026} introduced a novel ``additive
parametrization'' with an infinite descent argument; the preprint is
classified under General Mathematics (math.GM) rather than Number Theory
and awaits peer review.

\subsection{Methodological Contributions}

Our framework demonstrates the value of combining multiple search
strategies.  The key insight is that triple decomposition, which only
examines triples where two face diagonals are already integers, provides
orders-of-magnitude improvement over brute-force search.  The modular
sieve further pre-filters at negligible computational cost, and the
cross-domain concept evolution yielded actionable algorithmic insights:
the constraint-graph perspective led to the multi-modulus sieve achieving
99.8\% rejection, while the spectral gap framing motivated statistical
characterization beyond simple enumeration.

\subsection{Limitations}

Our search range ($10^5$) is modest compared to the state of the art
($10^{13}$, \citealt{matson2014}).  Our goal was to implement and
benchmark diverse methods, not to set new search records.  The power-law
regression has very low explanatory power ($R^2=0.025$), so asymptotic
conclusions should be treated with caution.  The quadratic sieve is too
aggressive as a standalone method---it rejects even Euler bricks because
it tests the space-diagonal condition.

% ============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================================

We have conducted a comprehensive computational investigation of the
perfect cuboid problem, implementing five distinct search strategies
within a unified framework.  Our combined search discovers 1{,}714 Euler
bricks with edges up to $10^5$ in 4.5~seconds ($>10^{6}\times$ speedup
over brute force), confirms the absence of perfect cuboids within this
range, and provides a statistical characterization of near-miss
distributions.

The weight of evidence---computational, algebraic-geometric, and
statistical---favors \textbf{non-existence} of the perfect cuboid.
However, this remains a conjecture: no proof has been accepted, and the
weak negative trend in near-miss scores leaves open the theoretical
possibility of a solution at very large edge magnitudes.

\subsection{Future Directions}

We identify six promising research directions, ordered by estimated
feasibility:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{GPU-accelerated sieve search.}  The multi-modulus QR
    sieve maps naturally to SIMD parallelism.  With modern GPUs achieving
    $10^{12}$ integer ops/s, the verified bound could be extended to
    $10^{16}$ \citep{matson2014,roberts2009}.

  \item \textbf{Brauer--Manin obstruction.}  Computing the Brauer group
    of the Stoll--Testa surface could definitively prove non-existence
    via a local-global obstruction \citep{stolltesta2010,stolltesta2025}.

  \item \textbf{Triangular remainder sieve.}  Implementing
    \citeauthor{yelle2026}'s construction \citeyearpar{yelle2026} as an
    empirical sieve would either yield a powerful new filter or identify
    counterexamples pinpointing gaps in the proof.

  \item \textbf{Formal verification.}  Formalizing the most promising
    proof attempt in Lean~4 would definitively validate or refute it
    \citep{lloyd2022,yelle2026}.

  \item \textbf{Machine learning for pattern recognition.}  Training
    graph neural networks on the constraint graph to predict near-miss
    scores could reveal structural patterns invisible to human analysis
    \citep{matson2014,rathbun2017}.

  \item \textbf{Lattice reduction.}  Reformulating the system as a
    lattice problem and applying LLL/BKZ algorithms may provide an
    alternative search paradigm \citep{vanluijk2000,stolltesta2010}.
\end{enumerate}

The perfect cuboid problem, at the intersection of number theory,
algebraic geometry, and computational mathematics, continues to exemplify
the productive interplay between theoretical insight and computational
power.
