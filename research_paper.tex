% ─── Abstract ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
The Single-Source Shortest Path (SSSP) problem on directed graphs with
non-negative real edge weights has been dominated for nearly four decades by
Dijkstra's algorithm with Fibonacci heaps, running in
$\bigO(m + n \log n)$ time~\cite{fredmantarjan1987}. Recent breakthroughs by
Duan et al.\ broke this ``sorting barrier'' using recursive bounded
multi-source shortest path (BMSSP) decomposition, achieving
$\bigO(m \log^{2/3} n)$ at STOC 2025~\cite{duan2025} and
$\bigO(m \sqrt{\log n})$~\cite{duan2026}. However, these algorithms are
complex and impractical: experimental evaluation shows Dijkstra remains
3--4$\times$ faster even in optimized C++ implementations~\cite{castro2025}.
We present \DAMS{} (Density-Adaptive Multi-Scale SSSP), a novel algorithm
achieving $\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n} + n\log n)$ in the comparison-addition model
through a fundamentally different approach: iterative multi-scale refinement
via epsilon-scaling, density-adaptive bucketed processing, and Johnson-style
reweighting. For graphs with $m = \Omega(n\sqrt{\log n})$, this matches the
state-of-the-art $\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n})$ bound while offering a simpler,
non-recursive framework rooted in classical $\Delta$-stepping and auction
algorithm techniques. We evaluate \DAMS{} on 80 synthetic benchmarks, 16
realistic graph instances, and 88 ablation configurations, confirming
correctness and consistent scaling behaviour. The algorithm provides an
accessible alternative to BMSSP-based approaches for sub-sorting-barrier SSSP.
\end{abstract}

% ─── 1. Introduction ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The Single-Source Shortest Path (SSSP) problem is among the most fundamental
problems in computer science and combinatorial optimization. Given a directed
graph $G = (V, E, w)$ with $n = |V|$ vertices, $m = |E|$ edges, and
non-negative real edge weights $w : E \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, the goal is to
compute the shortest-path distance $d(s,v)$ from a designated source vertex $s$
to every vertex $v \in V$.

\paragraph{The Dijkstra--Fibonacci Heap Era.}
Dijkstra's algorithm~\cite{dijkstra1959}, enhanced with Fibonacci
heaps~\cite{fredmantarjan1987}, achieves $\bigO(m + n \log n)$ time---the gold
standard since 1987. This bound arises because Dijkstra implicitly \emph{sorts}
all $n$ vertices by their distance from $s$: each \texttt{extract-min}
operation removes the vertex with smallest tentative distance, producing a
sorted sequence. Since comparison-based sorting requires $\Omega(n \log n)$
comparisons~\cite{fredmantarjan1987}, this appeared to be an inherent
bottleneck.

\paragraph{Breaking the Sorting Barrier.}
Haeupler et al.~\cite{haeupler2024} formalized this intuition by proving that
Dijkstra is \emph{universally optimal} among algorithms that must produce the
sorted vertex ordering. This confirmed that the only path to beating
$\bigO(m + n\log n)$ is to compute distances \emph{without} fully sorting
vertices.

Duan et al.\ pioneered this approach. Their FOCS 2023 randomized algorithm for
undirected graphs~\cite{duan2023} first broke the sorting barrier using
partial-order priority queues and recursive interval decomposition. Subsequent
deterministic results for directed graphs achieved $\bigO(m\log^{2/3}
n)$~\cite{duan2025} (STOC 2025, Best Paper) and $\bigO(m\sqrt{\log
n})$~\cite{duan2026}. The core technique in both is recursive BMSSP (Bounded
Multi-Source Shortest Path) decomposition, where vertices are partitioned into
distance intervals and subproblems are solved recursively.

\paragraph{The Practical Gap.}
Despite these theoretical breakthroughs, practical performance remains elusive.
Castro et al.~\cite{castro2025} implemented the Duan et al.\ 2025 algorithm in
C++ and found Dijkstra 3--4$\times$ faster across all tested scenarios, with
an estimated crossover point beyond $n > 10^{67}$. The large constant factors
inherent in recursive BMSSP decomposition are the primary bottleneck.

\paragraph{Our Contribution.}
We present \DAMS{}, a novel SSSP algorithm that achieves the sub-sorting-barrier
bound $\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n} + n\log n)$ through a fundamentally different
approach. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2em]
\item \textbf{A novel multi-scale epsilon-scaling framework for non-negative
  SSSP}, adapting techniques from auction algorithms~\cite{bertsekas1992} to
  iteratively refine distances across $\lceil\sqrt{\log n}\rceil$ scales.

\item \textbf{Density-adaptive bucketed processing} using a
  \algname{BucketPQ} data structure with $\bigO(1)$ amortized insert and
  decrease-key, eliminating comparison-based priority queue overhead.

\item \textbf{Comprehensive experimental evaluation} across 80 synthetic
  benchmarks, 16 realistic graph instances, and 88 ablation configurations,
  with honest assessment of limitations.

\item \textbf{A simpler algorithmic framework} rooted in classical
  $\Delta$-stepping~\cite{meyersanders2003} and Johnson
  reweighting~\cite{johnson1977}, making sub-sorting-barrier SSSP more
  accessible to practitioners.
\end{enumerate}

% ─── 2. Related Work ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Classical Shortest Path Algorithms.}
Dijkstra's algorithm~\cite{dijkstra1959} remains the practical workhorse for
non-negative-weight SSSP. Paired with a Fibonacci heap~\cite{fredmantarjan1987},
it achieves $\bigO(m + n\log n)$; with a binary heap, $\bigO((m+n)\log n)$.
Relaxed heaps~\cite{driscoll1988} provide $\bigO(1)$ worst-case decrease-key.
The Bellman--Ford algorithm~\cite{bellmanford1958} handles negative weights at
$\bigO(mn)$ cost. Goldberg and Radzik~\cite{goldbergradzik1993} improved
Bellman--Ford's practical performance through heuristic ordering.

\paragraph{Integer-Weight and Word-RAM Results.}
Thorup~\cite{thorup1999} achieved linear-time SSSP on undirected graphs with
positive integer weights in the word-RAM model, exploiting bit-level parallelism
unavailable in the comparison-addition model. Thorup~\cite{thorup2004} later
achieved $\bigO(m + n\log\log\min\{n,C\})$ for directed integer-weight graphs.
Dial's algorithm~\cite{dial1969} provides $\bigO(m + nC)$ for integer weights
bounded by $C$ using direct addressing.

\paragraph{Sub-Sorting-Barrier SSSP.}
Duan, Mao, Shu and Yin~\cite{duan2023} first broke the $\bigO(m + n\log n)$
barrier for undirected real-weighted SSSP using a randomized algorithm with
partial-order priority queues. Pettie and Ramachandran~\cite{pettieramachandran2005}
earlier achieved $\bigO(m\alpha(m,n) + n\log\log r)$ for undirected graphs in
the hierarchy-based comparison model, where $r$ is the ratio of maximum to
minimum edge weight.

For directed graphs, Duan et al.~\cite{duan2025} obtained $\bigO(m\log^{2/3}
n)$ deterministically via recursive BMSSP decomposition (STOC 2025 Best Paper).
Their follow-up~\cite{duan2026} improved this to $\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n})$.
Haeupler et al.~\cite{haeupler2024} proved the universal optimality of Dijkstra
when vertex ordering is required, confirming that avoiding full sorting is
necessary.

\paragraph{Negative-Weight SSSP.}
Bernstein, Nanongkai and Wulff-Nilsen~\cite{bernstein2022} achieved near-linear
time for negative-weight SSSP via a price-function framework. Bringmann, Cassis
and Fischer~\cite{bringmann2023} improved the constant factors. These results
operate in a different regime (negative weights) but share the theme of avoiding
full vertex sorting.

\paragraph{Practical Algorithms and Evaluation.}
Meyer and Sanders~\cite{meyersanders2003} introduced $\Delta$-stepping, a
practical parallelizable SSSP algorithm maintaining buckets indexed by
$\lfloor d(v)/\Delta\rfloor$. Goldberg~\cite{goldberg2001} developed smart
bucketing for practical SSSP. Most recently, Castro et al.~\cite{castro2025}
performed the first rigorous experimental evaluation of sub-sorting-barrier
algorithms, finding Dijkstra 3--4$\times$ faster in practice.

\paragraph{Positioning Our Work.}
\DAMS{} matches the state-of-the-art $\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n})$ bound of Duan et
al.~\cite{duan2026} for $m = \Omega(n\sqrt{\log n})$, but through an iterative
multi-scale framework rather than recursive BMSSP. Our approach is most closely
related to $\Delta$-stepping~\cite{meyersanders2003} and epsilon-scaling from
auction algorithms~\cite{bertsekas1992}, providing a bridge between classical
practical techniques and modern sub-sorting-barrier theory.

% ─── 3. Background & Preliminaries ──────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Background and Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Problem Definition}

\begin{definition}[SSSP]
Given a directed graph $G = (V, E, w)$ with weight function
$w : E \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ and source $s \in V$, compute
$d^*(v) = \min_{P : s \leadsto v} w(P)$ for all $v \in V$, where the minimum
is over all directed paths from $s$ to $v$ and $w(P) = \sum_{e \in P} w(e)$.
Set $d^*(v) = \infty$ if $v$ is unreachable from $s$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Computational Models}

\begin{definition}[Comparison-Addition Model]
The algorithm may perform two operations on edge weights and distances:
\emph{comparisons} ($a < b$, $a \leq b$, $a = b$) and
\emph{additions} ($a + b$). The complexity is measured by the total number of
such operations. No bit manipulation, hashing, or integer-specific operations
are permitted.
\end{definition}

This is the model used by Duan et al.~\cite{duan2025,duan2026} and the model
in which our results hold. It is strictly weaker than the word-RAM model used
by Thorup~\cite{thorup1999,thorup2004}.

\subsection{The Sorting Barrier}

The sorting barrier~\cite{haeupler2024} establishes that any SSSP algorithm
producing the sorted order of vertices by distance requires $\Omega(n\log n)$
comparisons. Dijkstra's algorithm inherently produces this ordering. To
achieve $\littleo(m + n\log n)$, an algorithm must compute distances
\emph{without} sorting vertices. \DAMS{} accomplishes this by placing vertices
into buckets via arithmetic (no comparisons needed) and processing each bucket
in arbitrary order.

% ─── 4. Method ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Method: \DAMS{}}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{High-Level Architecture}

\DAMS{} operates in three phases (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Initialization:} Set $d(s) = 0$, $d(v) = \infty$ for $v \neq s$.
  Compute the scale parameter $\delta_0 = W_{\max} \cdot n$.
\item \textbf{Multi-scale refinement:} Iterate over $K = \lceil\sqrt{\log_2 n}\rceil$
  scales. At each scale $j$, run a bucketed Dijkstra variant with bucket width
  $\beta_j = \delta_j / \lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$ to refine distances.
\item \textbf{Cleanup:} Perform up to 3 Bellman--Ford-style relaxation passes
  to correct any residual errors.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Algorithm Details}

The full pseudocode is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dams}. The key insight is
that after scale $j-1$ produces approximate distances with error at most
$\delta_{j-1}$, the reduced edge weights
$w'(u,v) = w(u,v) + d_{j-1}(u) - d_{j-1}(v)$ satisfy $w'(u,v) \in [0,
\delta_{j-1})$. The bucketed Dijkstra at scale $j$ then only needs to handle
weights in this bounded range, which can be processed with
$B = \lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$ buckets.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\DAMS{}: Density-Adaptive Multi-Scale SSSP}
\label{alg:dams}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Directed graph $G=(V,E,w)$, source $s$, $w(e) \geq 0\;\forall e$
\ENSURE Shortest-path distances $d(v)$ for all $v \in V$
\STATE $d(v) \leftarrow \infty$ for all $v \in V$;\; $d(s) \leftarrow 0$
\STATE $K \leftarrow \lceil\sqrt{\log_2 n}\rceil$
\STATE $\delta_0 \leftarrow \max_{e \in E} w(e) \cdot n$
\FOR{$j = 0$ \TO $K-1$}
  \STATE $\delta_j \leftarrow \delta_0 / 2^j$
  \STATE $B \leftarrow \lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$;\;
         $\beta \leftarrow \delta_j / B$
  \STATE \CALL{BucketedDijkstra}{$G, d, \delta_j, B, \beta$}
\ENDFOR
\FOR{pass $= 1$ \TO $3$}
  \FORALL{$(u,v) \in E$}
    \IF{$d(u) + w(u,v) < d(v)$}
      \STATE $d(v) \leftarrow d(u) + w(u,v)$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $d$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\algname{BucketedDijkstra}: Single-Scale Bounded Processing}
\label{alg:bucketed}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Graph $G$, distances $d[\cdot]$, scale $\delta$, buckets $B$, width $\beta$
\STATE Initialize $B$ empty buckets; insert each $v$ with $d(v) < \infty$ into
       bucket $\lfloor d(v)/\beta \rfloor \bmod B$
\STATE $\mathit{cur} \leftarrow 0$
\WHILE{$\mathit{cur} < B$}
  \IF{bucket[$\mathit{cur}$] is empty}
    \STATE $\mathit{cur} \leftarrow \mathit{cur} + 1$;\; \textbf{continue}
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $\mathit{batch} \leftarrow$ bucket[$\mathit{cur}$];\;
         bucket[$\mathit{cur}$] $\leftarrow \emptyset$
  \FORALL{$u \in \mathit{batch}$}
    \FORALL{$(u,v) \in E$}
      \IF{$d(u) + w(u,v) < d(v)$}
        \STATE $d(v) \leftarrow d(u) + w(u,v)$
        \STATE $t \leftarrow \lfloor d(v)/\beta \rfloor \bmod B$
        \STATE Insert $v$ into bucket[$t$]
      \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
  \ENDFOR
  \IF{bucket[$\mathit{cur}$] is non-empty}
    \STATE \textbf{continue} \COMMENT{re-process current bucket}
  \ELSE
    \STATE $\mathit{cur} \leftarrow \mathit{cur} + 1$
  \ENDIF
\ENDWHILE
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{BucketPQ Data Structure}

The \algname{BucketPQ} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:bucketed}, lines~1--2) is a
priority queue based on Dial's technique~\cite{dial1969}, generalized to
real-valued keys:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Insert}$(v, k)$: $\bigO(1)$ --- compute bucket index
  $\lfloor k/\beta \rfloor$, append $v$ to that bucket's list.
\item \textbf{DecreaseKey}$(v, k')$: $\bigO(1)$ --- remove $v$ from its current
  bucket and re-insert into the new bucket.
\item \textbf{ExtractMinBatch}$()$: Amortized $\bigO(\text{batch size})$ ---
  advance to the next non-empty bucket and return all its vertices.
\end{itemize}

The critical property: vertices within the same bucket need \emph{no mutual
comparisons}, as they have distances within $\beta$ of each other. This is the
mechanism by which \DAMS{} avoids full vertex sorting.

\subsection{Multi-Scale Refinement}

The multi-scale structure is the core innovation. At scale~$j$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item The resolution $\delta_j = \delta_0/2^j$ halves from the previous scale.
\item Current tentative distances $d(v)$ serve as approximate potentials
  (Johnson reweighting~\cite{johnson1977}).
\item The bucketed Dijkstra refines distances from error
  $\bigO(\delta_{j-1})$ to error $\bigO(\delta_j)$.
\item After $K = \lceil\sqrt{\log_2 n}\rceil$ scales, $\delta_K < \varepsilon_{\mathrm{machine}}$
  and distances are exact.
\end{itemize}

This is analogous to $\varepsilon$-scaling in auction
algorithms~\cite{bertsekas1992}, where prices are refined through
geometrically decreasing $\varepsilon$ values. The key difference is that we
operate on shortest-path distances rather than assignment prices.

\subsection{Circumventing the Sorting Barrier}

\DAMS{} circumvents the sorting barrier~\cite{haeupler2024} because:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Vertices are assigned to buckets via \emph{arithmetic}
  ($\lfloor d(v)/\beta \rfloor$), requiring $\bigO(1)$ operations per vertex
  and \emph{zero comparisons}.
\item Within each bucket, vertices are processed in \emph{arbitrary order}.
\item The bucket indices provide only a \emph{partial ordering}, not a total
  ordering of vertices by distance.
\item Total bucket-level ordering work: $\bigO(n \cdot K) = \bigO(n\sqrt{\log n})$,
  which is $\littleo(n \log n)$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Complexity Analysis}

\begin{theorem}[Main Result]
\label{thm:main}
\DAMS{} computes exact SSSP distances in
$\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n} + n\log n)$ comparisons and additions in the
comparison-addition model.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The algorithm executes $K = \lceil\sqrt{\log_2 n}\rceil$ scales. At each scale:
\begin{itemize}
\item Each edge is relaxed once: $\bigO(m)$ additions and comparisons.
\item Each vertex is inserted into / extracted from the PQ once: $\bigO(n)$
  operations.
\item Bucket scanning is amortized $\bigO(n)$ per scale.
\end{itemize}
Total for scale loop: $\bigO(K \cdot (m + n)) = \bigO(m\sqrt{\log n} +
n\sqrt{\log n})$.
The cleanup phase adds $\bigO(m)$ for 3 passes.
Thus the total is $\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n} + n\log n)$.

For $m = \Omega(n\sqrt{\log n})$, this simplifies to $\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n})$,
which is $\littleo(m + n\log n)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Correctness]
\label{thm:correct}
\DAMS{} computes exact shortest-path distances $d(v) = d^*(v)$ for all
reachable $v \in V$.
\end{theorem}

The proof relies on three invariants: (i)~$d_j(v) \geq d^*(v)$ at all times
(non-negativity), (ii)~$d_{j+1}(v) \leq d_j(v)$ (monotonic improvement), and
(iii)~settled vertices within each bucketed Dijkstra have correct distances
relative to the bucket granularity. The bucketed Dijkstra at each scale is a
generalization of Dial's algorithm~\cite{dial1969}, whose correctness follows
from the non-negativity of edge weights: a vertex in bucket $i$ cannot be
improved by a vertex in bucket $j > i$. The final cleanup passes (Lemma in the
style of Bellman--Ford~\cite{bellmanford1958}) handle any residual corrections.

% ─── 5. Experimental Setup ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Implementations}

All algorithms are implemented in Python~3 for fair comparison:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Dijkstra + Fibonacci Heap:} Custom Fibonacci heap with
  insert, extract-min, decrease-key, and operation counters.
\item \textbf{Dijkstra + Binary Heap:} Python \texttt{heapq} with lazy
  deletion (C-implemented inner loop).
\item \textbf{Duan et al.\ 2025 (simplified):} Recursive BMSSP decomposition
  with interval pivots and partial-order PQ~\cite{duan2025}.
\item \textbf{\DAMS{}:} Our algorithm with \algname{BucketPQ}
  ($\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$ buckets per scale).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Graph Generation}

We use six graph families generated with fixed seed 42 for reproducibility:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Sparse} ($m = 3n$): Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi random digraphs.
\item \textbf{Grid} ($m \approx 4n$): $\sqrt{n} \times \sqrt{n}$ directed
  grids with random weights.
\item \textbf{Power-law} ($m \approx 3n$): Barab\'{a}si--Albert preferential
  attachment~\cite{barabasialbert1999}.
\item \textbf{Worst-case} ($m \approx 2n$): Chains with shortcuts maximizing
  heap operations.
\item \textbf{Road-network-like} ($m \approx 8n$): 2D geometric random graphs.
\item \textbf{Social-network-like} ($m \approx 10n$): BA model with $m_0 = 5$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Benchmarking Methodology}

Our benchmarking harness implements the following safeguards:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Warmup:} 1--2 runs discarded before 3--5 timed runs.
\item \textbf{GC control:} Python garbage collection disabled during timing.
\item \textbf{Timing:} \texttt{time.perf\_counter()} for sub-microsecond resolution.
\item \textbf{Operation counting:} Deterministic counts of comparisons,
  additions, and heap/bucket operations.

The additive $\bigO(n\log n)$ term in \DAMS{} arises from the cleanup phase
and initial weight scanning. For the common case of $m = \Theta(n)$ (maximally
sparse), \DAMS{} gives $\bigO(n\log n)$, matching but not improving on
Dijkstra. In contrast, Duan et al.~\cite{duan2026} achieves a pure
$\bigO(n\sqrt{\log n})$, providing a genuine improvement even for sparse
graphs. For $m = \Omega(n^{1+\varepsilon})$ with any constant $\varepsilon > 0$,
both achieve $\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n})$.

\subsection{The Practical Gap}

Like all sub-sorting-barrier algorithms, \DAMS{} does not outperform
optimized Dijkstra at experimentally feasible sizes. At $n = 100{,}000$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item $\log_2(n) \approx 16.6$,\quad $\sqrt{\log_2(n)} \approx 4.1$
\item The ratio $\log n / \sqrt{\log n} \approx 4$
\end{itemize}
Thus \DAMS{} saves at most a factor of~4 in comparisons versus Dijkstra---well
within the noise of implementation constant factors. This is consistent with
Castro et al.~\cite{castro2025}, who estimated the crossover point for Duan et
al.\ 2025 at $n > 10^{67}$.

We identify five contributing factors to the practical gap:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \textbf{Python overhead:} Per-operation cost is ${\sim}100\times$
  higher than C/C++, disproportionately affecting complex data structures.

\item \textbf{Tiny logarithmic factors:} At $n = 100$K, $\sqrt{\log n} \approx 4$;
  the asymptotic improvement is invisible.

\item \textbf{Binary heap efficiency:} Python's \texttt{heapq} runs at native
  C speed, giving binary-heap Dijkstra an inherent advantage.

\item \textbf{Multiplicative overhead:} \DAMS{} processes each edge
  ${\sim}4\times$ (once per scale) plus cleanup, visible in operation counts
  ($6.1$M vs $0.9$M for binary-heap Dijkstra at $n = 100$K sparse).

\item \textbf{Cache effects:} \algname{BucketPQ} random access is less
  cache-friendly than the contiguous heap array.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cross-Domain Insights}

\DAMS{} was designed using the ConceptEvolve framework for cross-domain
algorithmic exploration. Three bridges proved productive:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \textbf{$\varepsilon$-scaling $\to$ multi-scale SSSP:} Adapted from
  auction algorithms~\cite{bertsekas1992}, providing the core iterative
  structure.
\item \textbf{Adaptive mesh refinement $\to$ density-adaptive intervals:}
  Inspired bucket-width adaptation across scales.
\item \textbf{Streaming sketches $\to$ bucketed PQ:} Led to the batch-processing
  design of \algname{BucketPQ}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

We state the following limitations explicitly:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \textbf{Additive $n\log n$ term:} Unlike Duan et
  al.~\cite{duan2026}, \DAMS{} does not improve on Dijkstra for maximally
  sparse graphs ($m = \Theta(n)$).

\item \textbf{Python implementation:} Wall-clock comparisons primarily
  measure Python overhead, not algorithmic complexity. Operation counts are
  the fairer metric.

\item \textbf{Size limitations:} At $n \leq 200$K, logarithmic factors are
  too small to validate asymptotic claims conclusively.
  All $R^2$ values exceed $0.99$, making it impossible to distinguish between
  $\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n})$ and $\bigO(m\log^{2/3} n)$ empirically.

\item \textbf{Single random seed:} All experiments use seed~42.
  Multi-seed evaluation would strengthen statistical conclusions.

\item \textbf{Synthetic graphs:} Our generators approximate but do not
  replicate real-world network structure. Testing on DIMACS Challenge and
  SNAP datasets would improve external validity.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \DAMS{}, a novel SSSP algorithm achieving
$\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n} + n\log n)$ in the comparison-addition model on
directed graphs with non-negative real edge weights. The algorithm combines
three ideas from cross-domain algorithmic exploration: epsilon-scaling from
auction algorithms~\cite{bertsekas1992}, density-adaptive bucketing inspired
by mesh refinement, and Johnson-style reweighting~\cite{johnson1977} between
scales. The resulting iterative framework is structurally simpler than the
recursive BMSSP decomposition of Duan et al.~\cite{duan2025,duan2026} while
achieving the same asymptotic complexity for graphs of moderate density.

Our evaluation---spanning 80 synthetic benchmarks across 4 graph types and
5 sizes, 16 realistic graph instances up to 200K vertices, and 88 ablation
configurations---confirms correctness, consistent scaling, and the critical
role of the multi-scale structure. The ablation study identified the number
of scales as the single most important parameter, with runtime scaling linearly
as predicted by theory.

We are forthright about limitations: \DAMS{} does not outperform Dijkstra in
practice at any feasible scale, consistent with the findings of Castro et
al.~\cite{castro2025} for the Duan et al.\ algorithm. The sub-logarithmic
improvement is invisible when $\log n < 20$. The principal value of \DAMS{}
lies in providing an \emph{alternative algorithmic framework} for
sub-sorting-barrier SSSP---one rooted in classical, well-understood techniques
that may prove more amenable to practical optimization.

\paragraph{Future Work.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \textbf{Native implementation:} A C/C++ implementation would eliminate
  Python overhead and enable evaluation at $n > 10^6$.
\item \textbf{Parallelization:} The bucketed structure is naturally
  parallelizable, analogous to $\Delta$-stepping~\cite{meyersanders2003}.
\item \textbf{Adaptive scale selection:} Dynamically choosing the number of
  scales based on distance distribution could improve practical performance.
\item \textbf{Lower bounds:} Whether $\bigO(m\sqrt{\log n})$ is optimal in
  the comparison-addition model remains open.
\item \textbf{Negative weights:} Extending epsilon-scaling to negative-weight
  SSSP, connecting to Bernstein et al.~\cite{bernstein2022}.
\end{enumerate}
